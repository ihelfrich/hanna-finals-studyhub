ECON 205 — Problem Bank by Chapter

117 problems · MC + numeric + applied · matched to Pérez’s syllabus

Targeted drilling for cumulative final

How to use this page

This bank is organized by syllabus chapter so you can drill weak spots without rereading the whole textbook. Each chapter has 5 multiple-choice, 3 short-answer / numeric, and 1 longer applied problem — nine items total. The cumulative final pulls from Ch. 3–9 (pre-midterm, ~30%) and Ch. 11–19 (post-midterm, ~70%), so post-midterm chapters carry the same weight here.

Recommended cadence. Run 2–3 chapters per study session (60–90 minutes). Within a chapter, do MC first under a 90-second-per-question cap to gauge whether you have the concept. Then do the numeric problems with a calculator and pencil — write out the formula before plugging numbers, the way Pérez expects on the exam. Finish with the applied problem. Only after all nine are submitted do you check the Solutions section at the bottom of the page.

If you score 9/9, mark the chapter green and move on. If you score 7–8/9, revisit just the question(s) you missed and the matching cram-sheet line. If you score ≤6/9, re-read the relevant cram-sheet section, redo the chapter from scratch the next day, and only then move on.

Self-grading rubric

Score Action
9 / 9 Green. Chapter retired.
7–8 / 9 Yellow. Re-do the missed item; check cram-sheet line.
≤ 6 / 9 Red. Re-read cram-sheet section, return tomorrow, redo all nine.

Ch. 3 — Supply & Demand

Cram-sheet refresh: Equilibrium is where Q^D = Q^S. A demand shift moves P and Q in the same direction; a supply shift moves P and Q in opposite directions. Elastic demand (|\varepsilon|>1) means a price increase lowers total revenue.

Multiple choice (5)

  1. A binding price below equilibrium creates a:
      1. Surplus
      1. Shortage
      1. New equilibrium at a lower quantity
      1. Tax wedge
  2. If demand for coffee rises while a frost simultaneously cuts coffee supply, equilibrium price:
      1. Rises unambiguously
      1. Falls unambiguously
      1. Is ambiguous in direction
      1. Is unchanged
  3. Demand is unit-elastic when:
      1. Quantity does not respond to price
      1. A 1% price change produces a 1% quantity change
      1. Total revenue rises with price
      1. The good has no substitutes
  4. A normal good’s demand curve shifts right when:
      1. Income falls
      1. Income rises
      1. The good’s own price falls
      1. A complement’s price rises
  5. The law of supply states that, ceteris paribus, Q^S rises when:
      1. The good’s price rises
      1. Input prices rise
      1. Technology worsens
      1. Demand falls

Short answer / numeric (3)

  1. Demand: Q^D = 120 - 2P. Supply: Q^S = 4P. Find equilibrium P and Q.

  2. At P = 10 the market has Q^D = 80 and Q^S = 50. Is there a shortage or surplus, and of how many units?

  3. Using the midpoint formula, compute the price elasticity of demand when price rises from $8 to $12 and quantity demanded falls from 100 to 60.

Longer applied problem

  1. Scenario A new ride-share entrant enters Los Angeles. At the same time, gasoline prices spike 30%.
    1. Which curves shift in the ride-share market, and in which direction?
    2. Predict the qualitative effect on equilibrium P and Q.
    3. If demand is highly elastic, what happens to total revenue when price falls?
    4. Why is the magnitude of the price effect ambiguous?

Ch. 4 — Application of S&D

Cram-sheet refresh: Tax incidence falls more on the less-elastic side. A binding price ceiling creates a shortage and DWL; a binding floor creates a surplus and DWL. Subsidies are the mirror image of taxes.

Multiple choice (5)

  1. A $2 per-unit tax on sellers, with elastic supply and inelastic demand, falls primarily on:
      1. Sellers
      1. Buyers
      1. The government
      1. Foreign producers
  2. A binding price ceiling on rent will:
      1. Increase quantity supplied
      1. Eliminate deadweight loss
      1. Create a shortage
      1. Raise consumer surplus unambiguously
  3. The minimum wage is a price floor that binds when set:
      1. Below the equilibrium wage
      1. Above the equilibrium wage
      1. Equal to the equilibrium wage
      1. At zero
  4. A per-unit subsidy of $3 paid to producers shifts:
      1. Demand right by 3
      1. Supply right (downward) by 3
      1. Both curves equally
      1. Neither curve
  5. Deadweight loss from a tax is largest when:
      1. Both curves are perfectly inelastic
      1. Both curves are highly elastic
      1. The tax is zero
      1. Supply is vertical

Short answer / numeric (3)

  1. Demand: Q^D = 100 - P. Supply: Q^S = P. A $10 per-unit tax is imposed on sellers. Find post-tax buyer price, seller price, and quantity.

  2. Using the same market, compute tax revenue and the deadweight-loss triangle.

  3. A price ceiling of $30 is imposed in a market where free-market equilibrium is P = $50, Q = 100, with Q^S = 2P and Q^D = 200 - 2P. Compute the size of the shortage at P = $30.

Longer applied problem

  1. Scenario The state imposes a $1 per gallon excise tax on gasoline. Demand: Q^D = 200 - 10P. Supply: Q^S = 30P.
    1. Solve for the pre-tax equilibrium.
    2. Solve for the post-tax buyer and seller prices and quantity.
    3. Compute tax revenue.
    4. Compute the deadweight loss.
    5. Who bears more of the burden, buyers or sellers? Justify with elasticities.

Ch. 5 — Macroeconomic Concepts

Cram-sheet refresh: GDP measures market value of final goods and services produced domestically in a period. Real GDP holds prices fixed at a base year; nominal GDP uses current prices. The GDP deflator is 100 \cdot \text{Nominal}/\text{Real}.

Multiple choice (5)

  1. Which is counted in US GDP?
      1. A used car sold in 2026
      1. A new haircut purchased in 2026
      1. A 2025-built apartment resold in 2026
      1. A foreign-made car sold to a US consumer (full sale price)
  2. Real GDP differs from nominal GDP because real GDP:
      1. Excludes services
      1. Holds prices fixed at a base year
      1. Adds the trade balance
      1. Subtracts depreciation
  3. The GDP deflator equals 125. This means:
      1. Prices have fallen 25% since the base year
      1. Prices are 25% above the base year
      1. Real output grew 25%
      1. The economy is in deflation
  4. A Toyota assembled in Kentucky and sold in the US is counted in:
      1. Japanese GDP only
      1. US GDP
      1. Neither country’s GDP
      1. Both countries’ GDP
  5. Intermediate goods are excluded from GDP to avoid:
      1. Imports
      1. Double counting
      1. Depreciation
      1. Inflation

Short answer / numeric (3)

  1. Nominal GDP = $22 trillion; real GDP = $20 trillion. Compute the GDP deflator.

  2. Year 1: deflator = 100. Year 2: deflator = 104. Year 3: deflator = 109. Compute year-2 and year-3 inflation rates.

  3. An economy produces only apples and bread. Year 1 (base): 100 apples at $1, 50 bread at $2. Year 2: 110 apples at $1.20, 60 bread at $2.50. Compute year-2 nominal GDP, real GDP, and the deflator.

Longer applied problem

  1. Scenario A small country produces only laptops and haircuts. Year 1 (base): 200 laptops at $1,000; 1,000 haircuts at $20. Year 2: 220 laptops at $1,100; 1,050 haircuts at $22.
    1. Compute year-1 and year-2 nominal GDP.
    2. Compute year-2 real GDP (base = year 1).
    3. Compute the year-2 GDP deflator.
    4. Compute year-2 inflation.
    5. Did real output grow? By what percent?

Ch. 6 — National Accounts

Cram-sheet refresh: Y = C + I + G + NX. Private saving S_p = Y - C - T; public saving S_g = T - G; total saving S = Y - C - G. In an open economy S = I + NX.

Multiple choice (5)

  1. The expenditure approach to GDP is:
      1. Y = C + I + G + NX
      1. Wages + rents + interest + profits
      1. Sum of value added
      1. Y = M \cdot V
  2. Public saving is:
      1. T - G
      1. G - T
      1. Y - C
      1. S_p + S_g
  3. In a closed economy at equilibrium:
      1. S = I
      1. S = NX
      1. S = G
      1. S = C
  4. If S = 1{,}000 and I = 1{,}300 in an open economy, then NX equals:
      1. +300
      1. –300
      1. 0
      1. +2,300
  5. Government purchases G in national accounts EXCLUDES:
      1. Salaries of soldiers
      1. Roads built by the federal government
      1. Social Security transfer payments
      1. State school construction

Short answer / numeric (3)

  1. Given C = 12{,}000, I = 3{,}000, G = 4{,}000, X = 2{,}000, M = 2{,}500. Compute GDP.

  2. In a closed economy, Y = 18{,}000, C = 12{,}000, T = 3{,}000, G = 4{,}000. Compute private, public, and total saving.

  3. Open economy: Y = 20{,}000, C = 13{,}000, G = 3{,}500, I = 3{,}000. Compute NX using the saving identity.

Longer applied problem

  1. Scenario Country Z reports: Y = 25{,}000; C = 16{,}000; I = 4{,}000; G = 5{,}000; X = 3{,}000; M = 3{,}000; T = 4{,}500.
    1. Verify GDP from the expenditure side.
    2. Compute S_p, S_g, total S.
    3. Confirm S = I + NX.
    4. Is the government running a surplus or deficit?
    5. Is the country a net lender or net borrower internationally?

Ch. 7 — Spending Shares & Investment

Cram-sheet refresh: S_p + S_g = I + NX in an open economy. A government deficit (S_g < 0) tends to crowd out I or NX unless private saving rises one-for-one (Ricardian equivalence).

Multiple choice (5)

  1. An increase in the government deficit, with private saving constant and NX fixed, must:
      1. Raise I
      1. Lower I
      1. Leave I unchanged
      1. Raise C
  2. In the loanable-funds market, a government deficit shifts:
      1. Demand for funds right
      1. Supply of funds right
      1. Demand for funds left
      1. Neither curve
  3. Ricardian equivalence states that:
      1. Government deficits raise private consumption
      1. Households offset government deficits by raising private saving
      1. Public and private saving are unrelated
      1. Inflation equals money growth
  4. Gross investment minus depreciation equals:
      1. Net exports
      1. Net investment
      1. Public saving
      1. Private saving
  5. In US national accounts, residential construction is counted in:
      1. C
      1. I
      1. G
      1. NX

Short answer / numeric (3)

  1. S_p = 2{,}500, S_g = -800, NX = -300. Compute I from the saving identity.

  2. Given Y = 22{,}000, C = 14{,}500, G = 4{,}200, T = 4{,}000. Compute S_p, S_g, and S.

  3. Closed economy: S = 3{,}000, demand for loanable funds is I = 4{,}500 - 200r (where r in %). Find the equilibrium real interest rate.

Longer applied problem

  1. Scenario Country W has S_p = 3{,}000 initially, S_g = -500, I = 2{,}300, NX = 200.
    1. Verify the saving identity.
    2. The government cuts taxes by $500, raising the deficit to $1{,}000. Households are non-Ricardian and raise S_p by $100.
    3. If NX falls by $100 and the rest must come from I, what is the new I?
    4. Show in words how this is “crowding out” via the loanable-funds market.
    5. Under full Ricardian equivalence, what would S_p have done, and what would I have been?

Ch. 8 — Labor Market

Cram-sheet refresh: LF = E + U, u = U/LF, LFPR = LF/\text{adult pop}. Frictional + structural unemployment ≈ natural rate (NAIRU). Cyclical unemployment is the deviation from NAIRU.

Multiple choice (5)

  1. A worker laid off because demand for their industry has structurally declined is classified as:
      1. Frictional
      1. Structural
      1. Cyclical
      1. Out of labor force
  2. The labor force participation rate is:
      1. E / \text{adult population}
      1. LF / \text{adult population}
      1. U / LF
      1. E / LF
  3. A discouraged worker who stops searching:
      1. Raises measured unemployment
      1. Lowers measured unemployment
      1. Has no effect
      1. Raises labor force participation
  4. NAIRU is roughly:
      1. Zero
      1. Frictional + structural unemployment
      1. Cyclical unemployment
      1. Always above 10%
  5. The unemployment rate falls when:
      1. Workers exit the labor force
      1. Workers move from U to E
      1. Both (a) and (b)
      1. Population grows

Short answer / numeric (3)

  1. Adult population = 250 million; E = 152 million; U = 8 million. Compute u, LFPR, and EPOP (employment-to-population).

  2. Last month u = 4.5\%. This month 1 million unemployed left the labor force; everyone else stayed put; LF fell to 159 million from 160 million. Compute the new u.

  3. If E = 144 million and u = 4\%, find the size of the labor force and the number unemployed.

Longer applied problem

  1. Scenario Adult population = 260 million. Initially: E = 158 million, U = 6.5 million.
    1. Compute u, LFPR, EPOP.
    2. A recession hits: 3 million workers move from E to U. Recompute u.
    3. After 6 months, 1.5 million of those unemployed give up searching and exit LF. Recompute u and LFPR.
    4. Did u rise or fall in step (c), and is this a good signal about the labor market? Explain.
    5. Identify which type of unemployment dominates in steps (b) and (c).

Ch. 9 — Production & Growth

Cram-sheet refresh: Cobb-Douglas Y = A K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha} exhibits CRS in K, L jointly and DMR in each separately. TFP A captures technology. Solow steady state has K/L stable; convergence: poor countries grow faster, conditional on institutions.

Multiple choice (5)

  1. In Y = A K^{0.5} L^{0.5}, doubling only K raises Y by a factor of:
      1. 2
      1. \sqrt{2}
      1. 4
      1. 1.25
  2. Diminishing marginal returns to capital implies:
      1. Each extra unit of K adds less output than the prior unit
      1. Y falls when K rises
      1. Labor productivity is constant
      1. Returns to scale are decreasing
  3. Total factor productivity A best captures:
      1. The capital stock
      1. The labor force
      1. Technology and efficiency
      1. Government spending
  4. The Solow model predicts conditional convergence because:
      1. Poor countries always have higher saving rates
      1. Poor countries have higher marginal product of capital
      1. Rich countries have lower TFP
      1. Trade equalizes wages
  5. Capital deepening means:
      1. Rising K/L per worker
      1. Rising population
      1. Falling savings rate
      1. Rising depreciation

Short answer / numeric (3)

  1. Y = K^{0.5} L^{0.5}, K = 100, L = 64. Compute Y.

  2. With the same production function, both K and L double. Compute the new Y and verify CRS.

  3. In a Solow model with s = 0.20, \delta = 0.05, g_L = 0, A = 1, \alpha = 0.5. Steady-state per-worker capital satisfies s k^{\alpha} = \delta k. Solve for k^* and y^* = (k^*)^{0.5}.

Longer applied problem

  1. Scenario Two countries share Y = K^{0.5}L^{0.5}. Country A: K = 400, L = 100. Country B: K = 100, L = 100.
    1. Compute Y and Y/L for each.
    2. Country B doubles its capital to 200 (catching up). Compute the new Y_B and Y_B/L.
    3. Country A doubles its capital to 800. Compute the new Y_A and Y_A/L.
    4. Which country gained more output per worker from doubling K, and what does this illustrate about convergence?
    5. Suppose Country B raises TFP from 1 to 1.5 instead of doubling K. Recompute Y_B/L at the original K = 100, L = 100. Compare.

Ch. 11 — Money & Inflation

Cram-sheet refresh: MV = PY. With V and Y stable in the long run, \%\Delta M \approx \pi. Fisher: 1+i = (1+r)(1+\pi^e), approx i \approx r + \pi^e. Hyperinflation usually arises from monetizing fiscal deficits.

Multiple choice (5)

  1. The quantity theory predicts that, in the long run, money growth changes:
      1. Real GDP
      1. The price level
      1. Real interest rates
      1. Productivity
  2. If i = 6\% and \pi^e = 4\%, the approximate real rate is:
      1. 10%
      1. 2%
      1. –2%
      1. 24%
  3. Unexpectedly high inflation tends to redistribute wealth from:
      1. Debtors to creditors
      1. Creditors to debtors
      1. Government to private sector
      1. Workers to firms
  4. Hyperinflation is most often caused by:
      1. A productive boom
      1. Monetization of large fiscal deficits
      1. Trade surpluses
      1. Capital outflows
  5. “Shoe-leather” costs of inflation refer to:
      1. The cost of changing menus
      1. The cost of holding less cash and visiting the bank more often
      1. Tax distortions
      1. Income redistribution

Short answer / numeric (3)

  1. M = \$2 trillion, V = 6, real GDP Y = \$20 trillion. Compute the price level P implied by MV = PY.

  2. Money grows 9% per year, real GDP grows 3%, and velocity is constant. Predict the inflation rate.

  3. Use the EXACT Fisher equation: i = 8\%, \pi^e = 5\%. Compute r to two decimals.

Longer applied problem

  1. Scenario Country Q has M = \$500 B, V = 4, real GDP = $1{,}000 B. Last year inflation was 4%; the central bank wants 2% inflation next year, with real GDP forecast to grow 3%.
    1. Compute the current price level.
    2. Using growth-rate form, what money-growth rate is consistent with 2% inflation given 3% real growth (V constant)?
    3. If instead M grows 12% with V and real growth as in (b), compute predicted inflation.
    4. Suppose nominal i is 7% and expected inflation jumps from 2% to 5%. What happens to the realized real rate, and who gains on a fixed-rate loan?
    5. Briefly explain why credible central banks can sometimes lower inflation expectations without a recession.

Ch. 12–13 — Economic Fluctuations & AD/AS

Cram-sheet refresh: AD slopes down (wealth, interest-rate, exchange-rate effects). SRAS slopes up (sticky wages/prices); LRAS is vertical at Y^*. Demand shock: P and Y co-move. Supply shock: P and Y move opposite.

Multiple choice (5)

  1. The AD curve slopes down because of all EXCEPT:
      1. Wealth effect
      1. Interest-rate effect
      1. Exchange-rate effect
      1. Diminishing marginal product of labor
  2. LRAS is vertical because:
      1. Prices are perfectly flexible in the long run, so output equals potential
      1. Wages are sticky in the long run
      1. Money is non-neutral
      1. The Fed pegs Y
  3. A negative AD shock (e.g., consumer pessimism) in the short run produces:
      1. P\downarrow, Y\downarrow
      1. P\uparrow, Y\downarrow
      1. P\downarrow, Y\uparrow
      1. P\uparrow, Y\uparrow
  4. Long-run self-correction after a negative AD shock works through:
      1. AD shifting back to the right automatically
      1. SRAS shifting right as wages and prices fall
      1. LRAS shifting left
      1. Government spending rising mechanically
  5. A positive supply shock (e.g., productivity gain) shifts:
      1. AD right
      1. SRAS right and LRAS right
      1. AD left
      1. Only LRAS left

Short answer / numeric (3)

  1. Currently Y = 19{,}500, Y^* = 20{,}000. Compute the output gap (sign and percent).

  2. Okun’s rule of thumb: \Delta u \approx -0.5 \cdot (\text{output gap}\%). With a –4% gap, predict the cyclical-unemployment increase relative to NAIRU.

  3. A 5% drop in oil prices is estimated to shift SRAS right enough to lower the price level by 1.5% in the short run. If AD is unchanged, predict the qualitative direction of Y in the short run.

Longer applied problem

  1. Scenario A sudden surge of consumer pessimism cuts C by $300 B. The economy was at Y^* = \$20 T with u = 4.5\% and \pi = 2\%.
    1. Which curve shifts and in which direction?
    2. Predict short-run effects on P, Y, u.
    3. If the central bank holds policy fixed, describe the long-run self-correction mechanism.
    4. Sketch (verbally) the short-run and long-run equilibria on AD/SRAS/LRAS.
    5. Identify two real-world frictions that slow long-run self-correction.

Ch. 14 — Application of Fluctuations

Cram-sheet refresh: Stagflation = supply-shock signature: P\uparrow, Y\downarrow, u\uparrow. Demand-shock recoveries are easier (policy-friendly direction). Keynesians emphasize demand-side stimulus; classicals emphasize price flexibility and supply.

Multiple choice (5)

  1. Stagflation is characterized by:
      1. Falling P and rising Y
      1. Rising P and falling Y
      1. Stable P and stable Y
      1. Rising P and rising Y
  2. Faced with stagflation from an oil shock, an inflation-targeting central bank typically faces a tension because:
      1. Both inflation and unemployment call for the same direction of policy
      1. Easing fights u but worsens \pi; tightening fights \pi but worsens u
      1. Fiscal policy alone resolves it
      1. The Phillips curve has shifted left
  3. Keynesians stress:
      1. Sticky prices and demand management
      1. Long-run money neutrality
      1. Rational expectations only
      1. Real business cycle shocks
  4. After a negative AD shock with no policy response, prices and wages fall, eventually shifting:
      1. AD right
      1. SRAS right
      1. LRAS right
      1. Both AD and LRAS
  5. Real business cycle theory attributes most fluctuations to:
      1. AD shocks
      1. Productivity (supply) shocks
      1. Money supply shocks alone
      1. Government deficits

Short answer / numeric (3)

  1. An oil shock raises the price level by 3% and lowers Y by 2% relative to potential. Using Okun (coefficient 0.5), predict \Delta u above NAIRU.

  2. If \pi rises from 2% to 5% while Y falls 1% in one quarter, identify which type of shock (supply or demand) is consistent.

  3. A Keynesian model with MPC = 0.75 and no income tax. Compute the spending multiplier and the increase in Y from a $200 B rise in G.

Longer applied problem

  1. Scenario A geopolitical event spikes global oil prices 40% in one month.
    1. Identify the curve shift and direction in AD/AS.
    2. Predict short-run direction of P, Y, u.
    3. The Fed targets 2% inflation. Should it ease or tighten? Why is the choice difficult?
    4. If oil prices stay elevated for two years, what does the long-run AD/AS prediction become?
    5. Briefly contrast Keynesian and classical recommendations.

Ch. 15 — Fiscal Policy

Cram-sheet refresh: Spending multiplier (closed, no tax) = \frac{1}{1-MPC}. Tax multiplier = \frac{-MPC}{1-MPC}. Balanced-budget multiplier = 1. Open-economy multiplier = \frac{1}{1-MPC(1-t)+m}. Crowding out: G\uparrow \Rightarrow r\uparrow \Rightarrow I\downarrow.

Multiple choice (5)

  1. With MPC = 0.75, the closed-economy spending multiplier is:
      1. 1.33
      1. 3.0
      1. 4.0
      1. 5.0
  2. The tax multiplier is always:
      1. Larger in absolute value than the spending multiplier
      1. Smaller in absolute value than the spending multiplier
      1. Exactly equal to the spending multiplier
      1. Equal to one
  3. The balanced-budget multiplier (equal \Delta G and \Delta T) equals:
      1. 0
      1. 1
      1. MPC
