# ECON 205 Final Exam — Killer Traps Drill Pack Key

## A) Real vs Nominal Interest / Fisher

1. Ex post r ≈ 8% − 7% = 1%. Lender lost vs expectations: expected real return was 3% (8−5), realized only 1%. **Intuition:** unexpected inflation transfers from creditor to debtor.

2. Real return ≈ 4 − 4 = 0. Loan breaks even in real terms; lender earns no real income. **Intuition:** when realized inflation equals nominal rate, real return is zero.

3. Exact: $1.10/1.06 - 1 = 0.0377 = 3.77\%$. Approx: $10 - 6 = 4\%$. The approximation drops the cross term $r\pi^e$, which gets larger at higher π. **Intuition:** Taylor expansion error grows with magnitudes.

4. Bond price drops sharply (yields rise to reflect new expectations). Real return on existing bond falls — locked in 3% nominal but inflation is now 5%, so real return is roughly −2%. **Intuition:** unexpected inflation devastates fixed-income holders.

5. Wrong: it should be $r \approx i - \pi^e$ (subtraction, not multiplication). Exact form is $1+r = (1+i)/(1+\pi^e)$. **Intuition:** Fisher decomposes the nominal return into real reward + compensation for expected inflation.

## B) AD/AS — Supply Shocks

6. SRAS shifts right, LRAS shifts right (productivity raises Y*). SR and LR: Y↑, P↓ (lower price level if AD held constant). **Intuition:** good supply shock = both P and Y move favorably. Distinct from a demand shock.

7. SRAS shifts left (higher unit labor costs). SR: P↑, Y↓, u↑ (cost-push stagflation lite). **Intuition:** wage shock with no productivity offset = adverse SRAS shift.

8. Appreciation → X↓, M↑, NX↓ → AD shifts left. SR: P↓, Y↓. **Intuition:** appreciation acts like a contractionary AD shock through trade.

9. Drop in oil prices is a positive SRAS shift (lower input costs), not AD. Effect: P↓, Y↑. **Intuition:** test of supply-vs-demand identification.

## C) Crowding Out vs Crowding In

10. Fed tightens → r↑↑ (more than under accommodation) → I↓ more strongly → realized multiplier well below textbook 5 (e.g., 1–1.5). **Intuition:** central bank reaction undermines fiscal stimulus when targeting inflation.

11. At ZLB, r doesn't rise; central bank accommodates. Crowding out is minimal. Realized multiplier is high (often estimated 1.5–2.5 in slack ZLB conditions). **Intuition:** policy regime determines fiscal effectiveness.

12. Tax cuts still crowd out when (a) the deficit raises r in loanable funds, (b) Ricardian-equivalence-leaning households save the cut, (c) economy is at full employment. **Intuition:** "always crowd in" requires slack + accommodative monetary policy.

## D) Open vs Closed Economy Multipliers

13. m=0: $1/(1-0.8)=5$. m=0.2: $1/(1-0.8+0.2)=1/0.4=2.5$. **Intuition:** every dollar of induced spending leaks abroad; less round-trips back into domestic Y.

14. $1/(1-0.8 \cdot 0.75) = 1/(1-0.6) = 1/0.4 = 2.5$. **Intuition:** income taxes claw back disposable income each round.

15. Leakages: (a) imports (m > 0), (b) income taxes (t > 0), (c) crowding out via r↑, (d) Ricardian saving. Realistic multiplier might be ~1.5–2 instead of 5.

## E) Exchange-Rate Direction

16. (a) Hurts US exporters to Europe? Actually no — euro appreciation = dollar depreciation, so US goods are CHEAPER in Europe, helping US exporters. (b) Hurts European exporters to US (their goods now expensive). (c) Hurts US tourists in Europe (dollars buy fewer euros). **Intuition:** appreciation of foreign currency = depreciation of home currency.

17. Higher US π → dollar depreciates ~3% per year against euro long run (PPP). **Intuition:** to keep baskets equal in value, faster-inflating currency must lose nominal value.

18. Wrong. Appreciation makes exports MORE expensive abroad (foreigners need more of their currency to buy our goods). Mechanism: foreigners face higher price of our currency → higher price of our goods.

## F) Comparative Advantage with PPF

19. X: opp cost 1 food = 0.5 cars; 1 car = 2 food. Y: opp cost 1 food = 1 car; 1 car = 1 food. X has CA in food (0.5 < 1). Y has CA in cars (1 < 2). Mutually beneficial terms: between 0.5 and 1.0 cars per unit of food.

20. Z gains via specialization in its CA (lowest opportunity-cost) good and trade. Absolute advantage doesn't matter — what matters is the difference in opportunity-cost ratios. If they differ, mutual gains exist.

21. X fully specializes in food: produces 100 × 10 = 1000 food, 0 cars. Trades 60 food for 60 cars at 1:1. Consumption: 940 food + 60 cars. Compare autarky frontier where X with 100 workers can produce at most some mix; e.g., 940 food domestically requires 94 workers, leaving 6 workers × 5 = 30 cars autarky. Trade gives 60 cars vs 30 autarky → strictly better. **Intuition:** specialization shifts consumption beyond the PPF.

## G) Quantity Theory

22. Growth-rate form: $\%\Delta M + \%\Delta V \approx \pi + \%\Delta Y$. So $\pi = 12 + 1 - 4 = 9\%$.

23. $\%\Delta M = \pi + \%\Delta Y - \%\Delta V = 2 + 3 - 0 = 5\%$.

24. P doubles. Yes, this is the quintessential demonstration of money neutrality in the long run.

25. Long run: AD shifts right, but at LRAS (vertical) only P rises permanently; Y unchanged. Money is neutral in long run. Short-run output gains exist but dissipate as wages/prices adjust.
