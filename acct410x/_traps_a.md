## Section A: Indirect Method Cash Flow

**Q1.** Adjustments: **+$18,000 depreciation, −$7,000 gain.** Net adjustment = +$11,000.
*Logic:* Depreciation is non-cash, so add it back. The $7,000 gain is already inside Net Income but the *full* $25,000 cash inflow belongs in Investing — to avoid double-counting, subtract the gain from Operating.
*Why students miss this:* They treat "gain" as an inflow word and add it. The cash already shows up in Investing.

**Q2.** The $4,000 gain is a non-cash component of Net Income; the actual cash from the sale appears in Investing. Adding the gain to Operating would double-count the cash. **Correct treatment: subtract $4,000 from NI.**

**Q3.**
- NI: $80,000
- + Depreciation: +$9,000
- AR ↑: −$12,000
- Inventory ↓: +$5,000
- AP ↓: −$4,000
- Accrued Wages ↑: +$3,000
- **Cash from Ops = $81,000**

**Q4.** **False.** An AR increase means revenue was earned but cash *not yet collected*. Subtract from NI.
*Why students miss this:* "More sales" sounds positive. But cash flow asks "did we get the cash?" — not yet.

**Q5.** (a) Cost $40,000 − AccDep $30,000 = BV $10,000. Sold for $15,000 → **Gain $5,000**. (b) Operating section: **subtract $5,000** (non-cash gain). (c) Investing section: **+$15,000** cash inflow.

**Q6.** **C. Decrease in AR.** Customers paid down balances → cash came in.

**Q7.**
- NI $50,000
- + Depreciation $10,000
- + Loss on Sale $3,000 (non-cash; add back)
- AR ↓: +$6,000
- Inventory ↑: −$9,000
- AP ↑: +$4,000
- **Cash from Ops = $64,000**

**Q8.** Depreciation reduced Net Income but did not use any cash this period (the cash was paid when the asset was originally purchased), so we add it back to undo a non-cash deduction.

**Q9.** Most plausible: the company **collected a large amount of AR** (AR decreased) and/or **drew down inventory** (inventory decreased) — both convert prior-period accruals into current-period cash. Profits down on paper, but cash conversion improved.
*Why this matters:* This is the textbook "earnings vs. cash" story a manager must understand.

**Q10.** **A. Added back to NI.** A loss is a non-cash debit included in NI; remove its effect by adding back. The actual cash from the sale (if any) goes to Investing.

**Q11.**
- NI $200,000
- + Depreciation $30,000
- − Gain on sale $50,000
- AR ↑: −$10,000
- AP ↑: +$8,000
- **Cash from Ops = $178,000**
*Note:* The full $200,000 sale proceeds appear in Investing.

**Q12.** Most likely: **a large depreciation/amortization add-back** (e.g., $45,000) — the company has heavy non-cash expenses dragging NI to zero while cash flows continue.

## Section B: Multi-Product CVP

**Q13.** Longboard CM = $400 − $250 = $150. Shortboard CM = $200 − $140 = $60.
Weighted-avg CM = (0.30 × $150) + (0.70 × $60) = $45 + $42 = **$87 per unit**.

**Q14.** Total break-even units = $90,000 / $87 ≈ **1,034.5 units → 1,035 units (round up)**.
- Longboards: 1,035 × 30% ≈ **311 units**
- Shortboards: 1,035 × 70% ≈ **725 units**

**Q15.** The company doesn't sell only longboards — it sells the *mix*. Using only the higher CM understates how many total units are needed because the average CM across the mix is lower.
*Why students miss this:* They equate "more profitable" with "use this number" and forget that the mix dilutes the average.

**Q16.** Cupcake CM = $4 − $1 = $3. Brownie CM = $3 − $1.50 = $1.50.
Weighted-avg CM = (0.60 × $3) + (0.40 × $1.50) = $1.80 + $0.60 = **$2.40 per unit**.
Required units = ($30,000 + $12,000) / $2.40 = **17,500 total units**.
- Cupcakes: 10,500; Brownies: 7,000.

**Q17.** **Higher.** The mix shifted *toward* the lower-CM product (brownies), so the weighted-average CM falls and break-even units rise.

**Q18.** Weighted-avg CM (mix 1:4 → 20%:80%) = (0.20 × $20) + (0.80 × $5) = $4 + $4 = **$8**.
Total break-even units = $48,000 / $8 = **6,000 units**.
- A: 1,200 units; B: 4,800 units.

**Q19.** **False.** Weighted-average CM ratio is total CM ÷ total sales (or weighted by *sales-dollar* mix, not unit mix). Unit mix gives weighted-avg CM in *dollars per unit*, not the ratio.
*Why students miss this:* The two formulas look interchangeable; they're not. Unit mix → $/unit; revenue mix → ratio.

**Q20.** CM ratio = $80,000 / $200,000 = **40%**. Break-even sales $ = Fixed Costs / CM ratio = $50,000 / 0.40 = **$125,000**.

## Section C: Inventory Valuation Errors

**Q21.** EI 2025 understated by $8,000.
- (a) 2025 COGS: **overstated by $8,000** (because COGS = BI + P − EI; smaller EI → bigger COGS).
- (b) 2025 NI: **understated by $8,000**.
- (c) 2026 COGS: **understated by $8,000** (the wrong 2025 EI becomes the wrong 2026 BI).
- (d) 2026 NI: **overstated by $8,000**.
- (e) End of 2026 RE: **correct** — the two errors offset.

**Q22.** The understatement (or overstatement) of NI in Year 1 is exactly reversed in Year 2, so cumulative two-year NI — and ending Retained Earnings — is correct.

**Q23.** (a) Reported COGS = 40,000 + 200,000 − 35,000 = **$205,000**. (b) Correct COGS = 40,000 + 200,000 − 50,000 = **$190,000**. (c) COGS overstated by $15,000 → **NI understated by $15,000**.

**Q24.** **B. Understated by $5,000.** 2024 ending overstatement becomes 2025 beginning overstatement → 2025 COGS too high → 2025 NI too low.
*Why students miss this:* They focus only on the year of the error, forgetting it rolls forward as the next year's beginning inventory.

**Q25.** Even if cumulative profit nets to zero, period-by-period decisions (loan covenants, bonuses, valuations, taxes paid in Year 1 vs. Year 2) are based on the *reported* numbers — so the wrong numbers can drive real, irreversible business choices.

**Q26.** EI overstated by $12,000 at end of 2025:
- Inventory: **overstated +$12,000**
- COGS: understated → NI overstated → **RE overstated +$12,000**
- Total Assets: **overstated +$12,000**
- Total Equity: **overstated +$12,000** (balance sheet still balances)

**Q27.** Year 1: NI understated by $6,000. Year 2: the $6,000 reverses (so without the second error, cumulative would be 0). The Year 2 EI overstatement *adds* +$4,000 to Year 2 NI.
- Year 1 reported NI: **−$6,000 vs. true**
- Year 2 reported NI: **+$6,000 + $4,000 = +$10,000 vs. true**
- **Cumulative two-year reported NI is overstated by $4,000** (the unreversed Year 2 error).

**Q28.** Ending inventory is *understated* (destroyed goods aren't counted, and there's no separate write-off). COGS = BI + P − EI, so smaller EI → **COGS overstated** → NI understated.
*Note:* Proper treatment is a separate "Loss from Fire" expense, not buried in COGS.

## Section D: Bond Pricing

**Q29.** **Discount.** Coupon (4%) < Market (6%): the bond pays less than investors can earn elsewhere, so they will only buy it for less than face.
*Mnemonic:* "Coupon < Market = Discount."

**Q30.** **Premium.** Coupon (4%) > Market (3%): the bond is more attractive than market alternatives, so investors pay above face. Issuer received extra cash up front; investors earn the coupon premium back over time. Both sides earn the market rate in present-value terms — neither is "winning."

**Q31.** **Greater than $25,000.** Under effective interest, Interest Expense = (Carrying Value × Market Rate). The CV ($460,000) is below face, and the market rate (the rate at issuance) is higher than 5%, so the dollar expense exceeds the cash coupon. The difference *amortizes the discount upward toward face value*.

**Q32.** (a) Carrying value **rises** toward face value. (b) Interest Expense **rises each period** (because expense = CV × market rate, and CV is growing).
*Why students miss this:* They expect expense to be constant like a coupon. Effective interest produces a growing expense for discount bonds.

**Q33.** (a) Premium amortization = Cash interest − Interest expense = $6,000 − $5,400 = **$600**. (b) New CV = $108,000 − $600 = **$107,400** (carrying value falls toward face for premium bonds).

**Q34.** The company received the premium *up front* in cash. It paid back face plus coupons; the premium received offsets the "extra" — the issuer's effective rate equals the market rate at issuance. The premium isn't a loss; it's compensation for an above-market coupon.

## Section E: Secondary Traps

**Q35.** Cost $60,000, salvage $6,000, life 5 yrs.
**(a) Straight-Line:** ($60,000 − $6,000) / 5 = **$10,800/yr**. Year 1 = $10,800; Year 2 = $10,800.
**(b) DDB:** Rate = 2 × (1/5) = 40%. Ignore salvage in formula (just don't depreciate below it).
- Year 1: 40% × $60,000 = **$24,000**. Book value end Y1 = $36,000.
- Year 2: 40% × $36,000 = **$14,400**. BV end Y2 = $21,600.

**Q36.**
- *% of credit sales method:* Bad Debt Expense = 1.5% × $800,000 = **$12,000**. Entry: Dr Bad Debt Exp 12,000 / Cr Allowance 12,000. (Existing $2,000 balance ignored — this method is "income-statement focused.")
- *Aging method:* Target ending Allowance = $14,000; existing balance = $2,000 credit; "plug" = **$12,000**. Entry: Dr Bad Debt Exp 12,000 / Cr Allowance 12,000. (Coincidentally same number here, but the *logic* differs — aging adjusts the Allowance to a target; % of sales records a flow.)
*Why students miss this:* They forget aging requires backing into the expense from the desired ending balance, while % of sales ignores the prior balance.

**Q37.** (a) **CA**, (b) **LTL**, (c) **CL**, (d) **LTA** (intangible), (e) **E** (contra-equity), (f) **CL**.
*Trap:* (f) — the "current portion" of LT debt is reclassified to current.

**Q38.** Bank loan, cash held: Current Assets ↑ (cash) but the loan is *long-term*, so Current Liabilities unchanged.
- (a) Current Ratio: **rises** (numerator up, denominator unchanged).
- (b) Debt-to-Assets: depends on starting position, but generally **rises** (both numerator and denominator grew by the same dollar; if D/A < 1 originally, the ratio increases).

**Q39.** Cash collected for future service:
- **IS:** No effect (no revenue earned yet).
- **BS:** Cash ↑ $10,000; Unearned Revenue (liability) ↑ $10,000.
- **CFS:** Operating cash inflow +$10,000.
*Why students miss this:* They book it as revenue. "Collected" ≠ "earned."

**Q40.** Dividend declared, not paid:
- (a) Total Assets: **no change** (no cash leaves yet).
- (b) Total Liabilities: **+$20,000** (Dividends Payable).
- (c) Total Equity: **−$20,000**.
- (d) Retained Earnings: **−$20,000**.
- (e) Net Income: **no effect** (dividends are a distribution of earnings, not an expense).
