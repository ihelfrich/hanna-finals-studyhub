# Iran as the Regional Engine: Linking the Two Conflict Spheres of the Middle East

*Research deep-dive prepared for IR 362 (Politics of the Middle East, Prof. Fayez Hammad), USC. Last updated: May 2026.*

---

## 1. Executive Thesis

Iran's regional behavior since 1979 is best explained as a fusion of **revolutionary-Shia ideology (Khomeinism / *Wilayat al-Faqih*) with a hard-nosed strategy of forward defense and asymmetric deterrence** designed to compensate for a conventionally inferior military, hostile neighbors, and decades of US-led sanctions and containment. This dual logic is what links the two conflict spheres of the modern Middle East — the Israeli–Palestinian/Arab–Israeli sphere and the Gulf-security/Syrian–Israeli sphere. Tehran cannot effectively deter Israel or the United States from Iranian soil, so it builds layered deterrence outward through proxies and allies: Hezbollah in Lebanon, the Assad regime in Syria, Iraqi Shia militias (Kata'ib Hezbollah, AAH), the Houthis in Yemen, and Hamas/PIJ in the Palestinian arena. The Israeli–Palestinian conflict and the Gulf rivalry are therefore *not* two separate theaters: they are the two ends of the same Iranian deterrent rope, with Syria as the geographic hinge and Hezbollah as the operational fulcrum. Whether the rope still holds in 2026 — after Hezbollah's degradation, the fall of Assad, and the Twelve-Day War with Israel — is the central empirical question of this exam topic; the most defensible answer is that Iran's *forward-defense* model has been broken, but Iran itself remains the only regional actor with both the ideological project and the proxy network capable of keeping the two spheres linked, even in degraded form (Goodarzi 2009; Nasr 2007; Parsi 2017; Chatham House 2025; Soufan Center 2025).

## 2. Historical Timeline (1979 → 2026)

- **Feb 1979** — Islamic Revolution; Ayatollah Khomeini overthrows Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. Doctrine of *Wilayat al-Faqih* (rule of the jurist) institutionalized; "Death to America/Israel" becomes regime catechism.
- **Nov 1979** — US embassy hostage crisis, hardening US–Iran rupture for two generations.
- **Sept 1980 – Aug 1988** — Iran–Iraq War. Saddam invades; ~500,000+ dead; Iran emerges siege-conscious, with IRGC institutionalized as a parallel military and ideological arm.
- **1980** — Syria (Hafez al-Assad) backs Iran against Saddam — sole Arab state to do so. Birth of Iran–Syria alliance (Goodarzi 2009).
- **June 1982** — Israeli invasion of Lebanon ("Operation Peace for Galilee"). IRGC dispatches ~1,500 trainers to Bekaa Valley; consolidates Lebanese Shia factions into **Hezbollah** (Norton 2007).
- **1983** — Beirut barracks bombings (US Marines, French paratroopers); Hezbollah emerges as Iran's premier asymmetric arm.
- **1989** — Khomeini dies; Khamenei becomes Supreme Leader; Hashemi Rafsanjani president. Pragmatic phase begins.
- **2000** — Israel withdraws from southern Lebanon; Hezbollah claims "divine victory." Bashar al-Assad inherits Syrian presidency from father Hafez.
- **2003** — US invasion of Iraq topples Saddam; unintentionally empowers Iran's Shia Iraqi allies (SCIRI, Dawa, Sadrists). Vali Nasr's "Shia Revival" thesis (2007).
- **July–Aug 2006** — 33-day Israel–Hezbollah war; Hezbollah survives, claims symbolic victory, deepens missile arsenal.
- **2011** — Arab uprisings; Syrian civil war begins. Iran and Hezbollah commit forces and money (~$30–50bn over decade) to save Assad (NBC 2024; Carnegie 2024).
- **2014** — ISIS rise; Soleimani's Quds Force coordinates Iraqi/Syrian Shia mobilization. Iran's regional reach peaks.
- **July 14, 2015** — JCPOA signed (P5+1 + Iran). Iran caps enrichment at 3.67%, reduces stockpile by ~97%, accepts intrusive IAEA inspections in exchange for sanctions relief (CFR; CRS R46663).
- **May 8, 2018** — Trump withdraws US from JCPOA; reimposes "maximum pressure" sanctions. Iran begins phased breakout.
- **Jan 3, 2020** — US drone strike at Baghdad airport kills **Qassem Soleimani** (Quds Force commander) and Abu Mahdi al-Muhandis (PMF deputy). Esmail Qaani succeeds Soleimani; many analysts argue the network's coherence never recovered (INSS 2025).
- **Sept 15, 2020** — Abraham Accords (UAE, Bahrain–Israel); Iran framed as common adversary.
- **March 10, 2023** — China-brokered **Saudi–Iran rapprochement**: Beijing Agreement restores diplomatic ties after seven-year rupture; Iraq and Oman did much of the prior groundwork (Atlantic Council 2024; Carnegie 2023).
- **Oct 7, 2023** — Hamas-led attack on Israel kills ~1,200 Israelis; ~250 hostages taken. Israel launches Gaza war. Hezbollah opens "support front" Oct 8 across Blue Line.
- **April 1, 2024** — Israel strikes Iranian consulate in Damascus, killing IRGC Brig. Gen. Mohammad Reza Zahedi.
- **April 13–14, 2024** — Iran's first-ever direct missile/drone strike on Israel (~300 projectiles); largely intercepted (Wikipedia "April 2024 Iranian strikes").
- **Sept 17–18, 2024** — Israeli pager and walkie-talkie attacks on Hezbollah; thousands of devices detonate; ~37 killed, ~3,000+ wounded (Lieber Institute 2024).
- **Sept 27, 2024** — Israel kills **Hassan Nasrallah** in massive bunker strike on Beirut's Haret Hreik (NPR 2024).
- **Oct 1, 2024** — Iran's second direct strike: ~200 ballistic missiles at Israel; ~24+ penetrate (Wikipedia "October 2024 Iranian strikes"; CFR).
- **Oct 26, 2024** — Israel's largest direct strike on Iran since Iran–Iraq War; targets air defenses and missile production.
- **Nov 27, 2024** — Israel–Hezbollah ceasefire: 60-day phased Israeli withdrawal from south Lebanon; Hezbollah pulls north of Litani; LAF + UNIFIL + 5-state monitoring panel (US-led) (Times of Israel 2024; Washington Institute 2025).
- **Dec 8, 2024** — **Fall of Assad.** HTS-led offensive collapses regime in 12 days; Assad flees to Moscow. Iran/Hezbollah evacuate personnel; "land bridge" to Lebanon severed (Al Jazeera; Chatham House Dec 2024).
- **Jan 2025** — Ahmad al-Sharaa (formerly Abu Mohammad al-Julani, ex-HTS) becomes de facto, then formal president of Syria; constitutional declaration with 5-year transitional term.
- **Feb 4, 2025** — Sharaa publicly denounces Iran's regional proxies as a "strategic threat" (NPR 2025).
- **Aug 28, 2025** — E3 (UK, France, Germany) trigger JCPOA snapback; UN sanctions reimposed Sept 27, 2025 (CRS IF11583).
- **June 13–24, 2025** — **Twelve-Day War**: Israel launches Operation Rising Lion targeting Natanz, missile sites, IRGC commanders, nuclear scientists. Iran fires 550+ ballistic missiles, ~1,000 drones. US bombs three Iranian nuclear sites June 22. Trump-brokered ceasefire June 24 (Britannica "12-Day War"; Wikipedia).
- **Late 2025–early 2026** — Pakistan-mediated US–Iran talks; Pezeshkian suspends formal IAEA cooperation after strikes; Iran signals willingness to dilute 60% HEU for sanctions relief (Al Jazeera Feb 2026; Commons Library 2026).
- **Feb 28, 2026** — Renewed US–Israeli strikes on Iran (per House of Commons Library 2026 briefing); negotiations resume under Pakistani mediation.
- **April 2026** — Sharaa publicly states Syria will remain "outside the conflict" between Iran and Israel (Chatham House April 2026).
- **May 2026 (today)** — Iran's regional architecture is degraded but not eliminated; Iraqi militias and Houthis remain Tehran's most reliable instruments; nuclear file remains the central diplomatic/coercive variable.

## 3. The "Axis of Resistance" — Analytic Anatomy

### Definition
*Mehvar-e moqavemat* ("axis of resistance") is the Iranian term, popularized in the early 2000s, for the network of state and non-state actors aligned with Tehran in opposition to Israel and the US-led regional order. The term originated partly as an Iranian rhetorical inversion of George W. Bush's 2002 "axis of evil" speech and partly out of indigenous Khomeinist discourse on *moqavemat* (resistance) as both political identity and military doctrine (Chatham House 2025 "Shape-Shifting"; Wikipedia "Axis of Resistance").

### Membership (pre-Oct 2023)
- **Iran (IRGC, especially Quds Force)** — strategic patron and operational coordinator.
- **Syria (Assad regime)** — the only Arab *state* member; geographic hinge.
- **Hezbollah** — premier non-state member; Lebanon's most powerful actor.
- **Iraqi Shia militias** — Kata'ib Hezbollah, Asa'ib Ahl al-Haq, Harakat al-Nujaba, Badr; loosely organized under PMF umbrella.
- **Houthis (Ansar Allah)** — Zaydi Shia movement controlling north Yemen / Sana'a since 2014–15.
- **Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad (PIJ)** — Sunni Islamist, but tactical members; PIJ closer to Tehran than Hamas.

### Coordination mechanism
Until January 2020 the axis ran on what observers called the "Soleimani model": personalized hub-and-spoke command via Quds Force, with Soleimani serving as broker between Tehran, Damascus, Beirut, Baghdad, and Sana'a. After his killing, his successor Esmail Qaani was widely judged less effective; coordination shifted toward Nasrallah personally (until his death in Sept 2024) under the slogan **"unity of the arenas"** (*wahdat al-sahat*) — the doctrine that an Israeli action against any axis member could trigger a multi-front response (INSS 2025; CSIS "Iranian Networks").

### Tehran's purpose vs. local member purpose
| Actor | Tehran's view | Local view |
|---|---|---|
| Hezbollah | Forward deterrent against Israel; ideological vanguard | Lebanese Shia community defender; political faction; blocking force in Lebanese state |
| Assad's Syria | Land bridge; balance against Israel/Turkey/Iraq | Regime survival; access to Iranian credit and manpower |
| Iraqi militias | Pressure on US forces; sectarian protection | Domestic political-economic patronage; sectarian self-defense post-ISIS |
| Houthis | Cheap leverage on Saudi/UAE; Bab al-Mandeb chokepoint | Local insurgency vs. Saleh/Hadi/Saudi coalition; Zaydi revivalism |
| Hamas/PIJ | Palestinian pressure on Israel; Sunni cover for axis | National-liberation/Islamist project; resistance to occupation |

### Ideology vs. instrumental rationale
Khomeinism's universalist Shia revivalism is real but **not sufficient** to explain alliance choices: Hamas and PIJ are Sunni; Assad's regime is doctrinally secular Ba'athist; the Houthis are Zaydi (a distinct branch). What unites the network is **opposition to US/Israeli regional hegemony plus dependence on Iranian money, weapons, and training**. As Goodarzi (2009) argued of the Iran–Syria pillar: the alliance was always defensive, oriented against shared external threats (Israel, Saddam's Iraq, US, Turkey), not a confessional bloc.

### Empirical reality after 2024
By mid-2025 the axis was demonstrably **degraded but not destroyed**:
- Hezbollah lost its top three military leadership tiers, much of its precision-missile arsenal, and its claim to deterrence (Soufan Center 2025; INSS 2025).
- Assad's Syria, the geographic hinge, is gone; the Sharaa government is actively hostile to Tehran.
- The Iraqi militias and Houthis remain functional and have, in fact, become *more* central in Iranian planning post-2024 (CSIS "Axis Rising" 2025).
- The doctrine of "unity of the arenas" was, in practice, falsified: Hezbollah did not open a full second front during the worst of the Gaza war, and other axis members did not coordinate around Hezbollah's collapse.

The Chatham House 2025 study calls this "shape-shifting": the axis is reverting to its **local roots** rather than functioning as a synchronized regional bloc (Chatham House 2025; Century Foundation "Down but Not Out" 2025).

## 4. Iran–Syria Alliance — Sources

Goodarzi (2009) is the indispensable scholarly treatment. He argues the alliance is best understood as a defensive *strategic* partnership rather than a sectarian one. Each candidate explanation deserves separate weight:

1. **Sectarian (Alawite–Shia affinity).** *Weight: low to moderate.* The Alawi sect was given a *fatwa* of legitimacy as a branch of Twelver Shi'ism by Musa al-Sadr in the 1970s, useful for both sides. But Hafez al-Assad's Ba'athism was self-consciously secular and Arab nationalist, and Syria's pro-Iranian alignment in 1980 made *strategic* sense regardless of sect. Sectarian framing became more salient *after* 2011 when Iran mobilized Shia foreign fighters to defend Assad — i.e., it was *weaponized* rather than foundational (Goodarzi; Tandfonline 2025 on sectarian mobilization).

2. **Strategic (Israel, Iraq, Turkey).** *Weight: high.* Both regimes regarded Israel as principal adversary; both needed counterweight to Saddam's Iraq during the 1980s; both worried about Turkish/NATO penetration of the Levant. Syria's geographic position made it indispensable as Iran's land corridor to Hezbollah and the Mediterranean. This is the core Goodarzi thesis.

3. **Economic.** *Weight: moderate but rising over time.* Iran underwrote Syria with credit lines, oil deliveries, and reconstruction commitments after 2011; estimates of total Iranian outlay range $30–50 billion (NBC 2024). This was not the alliance's *origin* but became a heavy sunk-cost binding by the 2010s.

4. **Regime survival (post-2011).** *Weight: very high in the 2011–2024 phase.* When Assad's regime nearly collapsed, Iran intervened directly through IRGC, Hezbollah, and foreign Shia volunteers (Fatemiyoun, Zainabiyoun) — over 2,100 Iranian soldiers killed in Syria (Carnegie 2024). Iran's commitment was about preventing Sunni-Islamist takeover of the geographic hinge — i.e., it was strategic-existential, not creedal.

**Synthesis.** The alliance was *born* strategic, *deepened* economic, *intensified* sectarian-instrumental during the civil war, and *ended* (in its Assad form) when the regime's coercive base and its Russian/Iranian backers proved unable to sustain it against HTS in late 2024.

## 5. Hezbollah as Iran's Forward Arm

### Founding and sponsorship
Hezbollah was constituted in 1982–1985 from a fusion of pre-existing Shia movements (especially the militant wing of *Amal*, the Da'wa Party, and the Lebanese students of Najaf clerics) under the catalyzing pressure of Israel's June 1982 invasion. Roughly 1,500 IRGC trainers in the Bekaa Valley unified these factions ideologically around Khomeini's *Wilayat al-Faqih* and operationally around suicide-bombing/asymmetric tactics (Norton 2007; CFR 2024). The 1985 "Open Letter" formally announced the movement, declaring loyalty to Khomeini and demanding the end of "Western imperialism" in Lebanon.

### Role in Lebanese politics
- 1992 — first parliamentary participation; "Lebanonization" thesis (Norton): the movement is both Iranian instrument *and* a genuinely indigenous Lebanese Shia political force.
- 2008 — Doha Agreement gives Hezbollah de facto veto over cabinet decisions.
- 2018–2024 — Hezbollah and allies hold parliamentary majority; control telecommunications dossier, key ministries.

### Military capabilities ca. early 2023
Most published estimates put pre-war Hezbollah at ~100,000 fighters (active + reserve); ~120,000–150,000 rockets/missiles (mostly short-range Katyushas, but with growing precision-guided arsenal — Fateh-110 variants, anti-ship missiles, drones). It was widely considered the world's most heavily armed non-state actor.

### Post-Sept 2024 status
The pager/walkie-talkie attacks of Sept 17–18, 2024 disabled mid-tier command-and-control. The Sept 27 Beirut strike killed Nasrallah and decapitated the Shura Council; subsequent strikes killed Hashem Safieddine (presumptive successor) and most of the Radwan Force command. By the November 27 ceasefire, Israel claimed (and most independent estimates broadly agreed) that majority of Hezbollah's precision-missile inventory and southern Lebanon infrastructure were destroyed (ICCT 2024; Washington Institute 2025).

Naim Qassem assumed leadership in Oct 2024. By 2026 Hezbollah survives as a political and social force in Lebanon but its **deterrent value to Iran has been broken**: it cannot credibly threaten Israel with massive missile barrages, and the LAF is, for the first time in decades, deploying south of the Litani. The Lebanese state has cautiously begun reasserting authority over the southern arms file under US/French pressure.

### Strategic implication
Nasrallah's death and Hezbollah's degradation were the single most consequential blow to the axis. Hezbollah was the axis's **operational fulcrum**: the actor most capable of independent action, most ideologically committed, and most strategically connected to Tehran. With Hezbollah disabled and Assad gone, the axis's Levantine arm — the very arm that linked the Israeli–Palestinian sphere to the Gulf-security sphere — is functionally severed.

## 6. The 2024–2026 Collapse — What Changed

Five interlocking shocks broke the post-2003 Iranian forward-defense model:

1. **Assad's fall (Dec 8, 2024).** HTS, backed permissively by Turkey, executed an offensive that swept through Aleppo and Damascus in 12 days. Russia (preoccupied with Ukraine) and Iran (post-Soleimani, post-Nasrallah) did not surge support. Iran lost the *land bridge*; Sharaa's government in 2025 publicly denounced Iranian proxies and expelled IRGC personnel (NPR; Iran International 2025).

2. **Hezbollah's degradation (Sept–Nov 2024).** See §5. The November 27 ceasefire formalized Hezbollah's strategic defeat (Times of Israel 2024).

3. **Direct Israel–Iran exchanges (April 2024 / Oct 2024 / June 2025 Twelve-Day War).** Israel struck Iran's nuclear facilities and IRGC personnel inside Iran with impunity; the US bombed Natanz, Fordow, and Isfahan on June 22, 2025 (Britannica). This collapsed Iran's *deterrence-by-distance*.

4. **JCPOA snapback (Aug–Sept 2025).** UN sanctions reimposed; Iran's economic situation worsens further (CRS IF11583).

5. **Saudi–Iran rapprochement (March 2023).** Counter-intuitively, the *de-escalation* with Riyadh signaled Tehran's recognition that two-front pressure (Gulf + Israeli) was unsustainable. Beijing's mediation also changed the security architecture by inserting China as a face-saving guarantor (Atlantic Council 2024).

### Is Iran still the regional engine?
Two defensible answers:

- **"Yes, but degraded."** Iran remains the only actor with *both* the ideology and the surviving network (Iraqi militias, Houthis, residual Hezbollah, Hamas) to keep the two conflict spheres linked. Even after the Twelve-Day War, the Houthis kept attacks on Red Sea shipping going, Iraqi militias periodically struck US bases, and Iran retained 60% HEU stockpile (Soufan Center 2025).
- **"No, the model broke in 2024."** The forward-defense doctrine is empirically falsified: Hezbollah did not deter; Assad did not survive; the proxies did not synchronize; Iran absorbed direct strikes on its soil. Iran is now *one* regional power among several (Saudi Arabia, Turkey, UAE, Israel) rather than *the* engine (Chatham House 2025; INSS 2025).

The exam-ready synthesis: **Iran remains structurally central but functionally diminished.** It still links the two spheres because its proxies still operate at both ends, but the linkage is now thinner, more tactical, and increasingly dependent on Iraqi/Yemeni rather than Levantine pieces.

## 7. Linkage Logic — Why Iran's Posture Affects the Israeli–Palestinian Conflict

The mechanism by which Iran's behavior in Lebanon/Syria shapes Israeli decisions on Gaza/West Bank operates through five channels:

1. **Multi-front threat and Israeli planning.** As long as Hezbollah's missile arsenal was intact, Israeli operations in Gaza were constrained by the need to keep northern divisions in reserve. After Sept–Nov 2024, that constraint *eased*, which partly explains Israel's freer hand vis-à-vis Iran in 2025.

2. **"Unity of the arenas" doctrine.** Iran's ambition was to make any Israeli action against one front trigger costs on others. The Oct 7, 2023 attack and Hezbollah's Oct 8 "support front" was the first real test. The doctrine partially worked (sustained Israeli ground deployment in north) but also catalyzed Israel's decision to terminate Hezbollah as a deterrent — an outcome Tehran did not anticipate.

3. **Hostage and prisoner files.** Tehran's leverage over Hamas during hostage negotiations gave it indirect influence over Israeli political bandwidth.

4. **Saudi–Israel normalization.** Pre-Oct 7, the Biden administration was within weeks of a Saudi-Israeli normalization framework. After Oct 7, Riyadh paused and demanded a credible Palestinian-state pathway; the war made the political price of normalization without that pathway prohibitive (IEMed; Carnegie Apr 2025; ArabCenterDC 2025). Iran benefits asymmetrically from this pause: it cannot prevent normalization, but it can raise the cost of normalizing during active conflict — and Hamas's Oct 7 attack served exactly that function. Whether Hamas was directed by Tehran for this purpose is contested; most analysts agree Iran did not have advance operational knowledge but benefited strategically (Parsi 2017's framing of "treacherous alliance" applies).

5. **Iranian nuclear leverage.** Continued enrichment toward weapons-grade gives Iran a hedge: if its proxies are degraded, its nuclear ambiguity becomes the residual deterrent. This is why the Twelve-Day War targeted *both* missile production and nuclear facilities — Israel/US understood the substitution.

**Bottom line:** Iran links the two spheres less by direct military integration than by *maintaining strategic ambiguity* across both. Its proxies create costs on Israel's northern and Palestinian fronts; its nuclear program creates costs on the Gulf and global-energy front; the Saudi–Iran detente reduces, but does not eliminate, the southern Gulf dimension.

## 8. Counterarguments and Heterodox Views

A serious essay should engage these:

1. **"The axis is largely a US/Israeli construct."** Some scholars argue the bloc-like framing of "axis of resistance" overstates command coherence and understates each member's local agency. Hamas, Hezbollah, Houthis, and Iraqi militias each act for parochial reasons; calling them an axis serves Israeli/US framing more than empirical reality (cf. Chatham House 2025; Mansour, Shakeri, and Haid 2025).

2. **"Local agency dominates."** POMEPS-affiliated scholars (Lynch and contributors) emphasize that Hamas is not Iranian-controlled, Hezbollah's Lebanese constituency limits its room for maneuver, and the Houthis pre-existed Iranian patronage. The "Iran pulls strings" model overstates Tehran's centralization (POMEPS Studies; Lynch various).

3. **"Saudi–Iran rivalry is overstated."** Gause (2009) argued that the Gulf rivalry is structural-realist (two large unbalanced states) but *not* irreconcilable. The 2023 Beijing agreement vindicates this view.

4. **"Iran's regional 'strategy' is reactive, not engineered."** Trita Parsi (2017, *Losing an Enemy*) argues Iran's posture is largely a function of US containment and Israeli pressure; remove those pressures and the deterrent posture would relax. This is a structural-realist counter to the ideological-Khomeinist framing.

5. **"It was always going to fall."** Some critics retroactively argue the network was always fragile — over-leveraged on a single elderly leader (Nasrallah), a brittle Syrian regime, and a sanctions-stressed economy. The 2024 collapse confirms long-running structural weakness rather than novel rupture.

A graduate-level answer doesn't need to *adopt* these views, but should acknowledge that the standard "Iran is the engine" framing is contested and should be defended on the empirical evidence rather than asserted.

## 11. Three Exam-Ready Paragraph Templates (~200 words each)

### Template A — "Iran as the regional engine" (defense)
Iran has been the central engine of Middle Eastern conflict since 1979 because it is the only regional power that has fused a transnational revolutionary ideology — Khomeinism and *Wilayat al-Faqih* — with a deliberate strategy of forward defense through proxies. Conventionally inferior and surrounded by adversaries, Tehran built the so-called "axis of resistance" (Hezbollah from 1982, Syria from 1980, Iraqi militias post-2003, Houthis from the 2010s, Hamas and PIJ as tactical members) under Quds Force coordination, particularly under Qassem Soleimani until his January 2020 assassination. This network linked the Israeli–Palestinian conflict sphere to the Gulf-security sphere because Syria served as the geographic land bridge to Hezbollah, while Hezbollah's missile arsenal constrained Israeli freedom of action against Iran itself. Goodarzi (2009) shows the Iran–Syria pillar was strategic from the start; Norton (2007) shows Hezbollah's Lebanese roots ran deep enough to outlast individual leaders; Nasr (2007) places this within the broader "Shia Revival" unleashed by the 2003 Iraq War. Even after the 2024 collapse, Iran remains the only actor able to project pressure simultaneously into Lebanon, Iraq, Yemen, Gaza, and the Strait of Hormuz — which is what "regional engine" structurally means.

### Template B — "The 2024 collapse changes the picture"
The events of September 2024 through June 2025 broke the Iranian forward-defense model that had structured the region since the early 2000s. In sequence: Israel's pager and walkie-talkie attacks (September 17–18, 2024) decapitated Hezbollah's mid-tier; the September 27 strike killed Hassan Nasrallah; the November 27, 2024 ceasefire codified Hezbollah's withdrawal north of the Litani; HTS's twelve-day offensive ended Bashar al-Assad's regime on December 8, 2024, severing Iran's land bridge; and Israel's June 2025 Operation Rising Lion, with US strikes on Natanz, Fordow, and Isfahan on June 22, demonstrated that Iran could no longer deter direct attacks on its homeland. Each of these had been a load-bearing pillar of the "unity of the arenas" doctrine. The Soufan Center, Chatham House, and INSS converge on the assessment that Iran is now at its weakest regional position since 1979. What survives — Iraqi militias, the Houthis, residual Hezbollah, the nuclear program — is real but insufficient to recreate the integrated regional posture. The exam point: the *structural argument* about Iran linking the two conflict spheres still holds; the *empirical reality* of how thickly that linkage runs has changed.

### Template C — "Linkage logic: Lebanon ↔ Gaza ↔ the Gulf"
The clearest mechanism by which Iran links the Israeli–Palestinian and Gulf-security spheres is the doctrine known as "unity of the arenas," which Tehran articulated through Hezbollah and partner militias. Its premise was that Israeli action against any axis member — in Gaza, Lebanon, Syria, or Iraq — would impose multi-front costs, deterring Israel and indirectly constraining US Gulf posture. This doctrine was tested after October 7, 2023: Hezbollah opened a "support front" on October 8, holding northern Israel's military reserves immobilized for nearly a year. It also reshaped Saudi calculations — Riyadh, which had been weeks from a US-mediated normalization with Israel, paused talks because the political cost of normalizing during a Gaza war was prohibitive (IEMed; Carnegie 2025). Yet the doctrine's limits were exposed in 2024: Hezbollah did not unleash its full arsenal even as Israel ground it down, and other axis members did not synchronize defense of Hezbollah. The March 2023 Saudi–Iran rapprochement, brokered in Beijing, simultaneously cooled the Gulf dimension. Iran therefore links the spheres through *strategic ambiguity* — proxies on the Israeli front, nuclear leverage on the Gulf front — but the integration that "unity of the arenas" promised has, by 2026, been falsified.
