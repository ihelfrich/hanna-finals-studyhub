# The Abraham Accords and the Saudi–Israeli Normalization Track

*A research deep-dive for IR 362 (Politics of the Middle East), Prof. Fayez Hammad — cumulative final preparation.*

---

## 1. Executive thesis

The Abraham Accords are best understood as **simultaneous continuity and rupture**: continuity in the long Gulf-Israeli convergence around the Iranian threat that had been building quietly since at least 2007, but rupture in the *normative* architecture of Arab-Israeli diplomacy. From 1967 through 2002, the regional template — codified in UN 242, the Madrid framework, and most explicitly in the 2002 Arab Peace Initiative (API) — held that Arab normalization with Israel was the *reward* for ending the occupation and producing a Palestinian state. The Accords inverted that sequence. Normalization became the *vehicle*, not the prize, and the Palestinian file was demoted from precondition to footnote (Lynch 2024; Khalidi 2023). October 7, 2023 then forced a partial reversion: Saudi Arabia, the regional pricing power for any wider Sunni-Israeli accommodation, has since 2024 publicly re-attached normalization to a credible Palestinian-state pathway, even as the four signed Accords have survived intact in form (Carnegie 2025; INSS 2026). The defensible claim is therefore that the Accords represent an *attempted* paradigm shift — initially successful at the regime level, decisively rejected by Arab publics, and now stress-tested back toward something closer to the API equation, though without the API's text or its Palestinian-state-first sequencing.

## 2. The Arab Peace Initiative (API), 2002 — the prior framework

### 2.1 Origins and adoption

The API originated as a proposal floated by Saudi Crown Prince Abdullah bin Abdulaziz in February 2002, during the Second Intifada and amid intense U.S.-Saudi tension after 9/11. Abdullah communicated the kernel — full Arab normalization for full Israeli withdrawal — through a *New York Times* column by Thomas Friedman, then formalized it at the **Beirut Arab League Summit on 28 March 2002**, where it was adopted unanimously by all 22 League members (Britannica n.d.; Wikipedia, "Arab Peace Initiative"). The League re-endorsed it at the 2007 Riyadh Summit and again, with modifications, in 2017.

### 2.2 Core deal

The API's three operative demands on Israel were:

1. **Full withdrawal** from territories occupied in June 1967 — the West Bank (including East Jerusalem), Gaza, the Syrian Golan, and (ambiguously) southern Lebanon — implementing UNSC 242 and 338 and the "land-for-peace" principle of Madrid 1991.
2. **A sovereign Palestinian state** with East Jerusalem as its capital.
3. A **just resolution of the Palestinian refugee question** "in accordance with UN General Assembly Resolution 194" (the right-of-return clause, deliberately framed loosely to leave room for negotiated outcomes) (text reproduced in USIP n.d.; ECF n.d.).

In exchange, the Arab states would (a) consider the Arab-Israeli conflict ended, (b) sign a peace agreement with Israel, and (c) establish "normal relations" within a comprehensive peace framework.

### 2.3 Why the API became the regional template even without execution

The Sharon government dismissed the API at the time, and it never produced a negotiation. Yet for two decades it functioned as the **default Arab consensus position**, the rhetorical anchor every Arab regime cited when domestic publics or the OIC demanded a stance on Palestine. Rami Khouri's well-known framing (Khouri 2013, *Lost in the Translation*) is that the API was the most generous unified Arab offer ever made — recognition by 57 OIC states, not just the front-line Arab ones — and that its repeated dismissal by Israel and Washington left a normative vacuum the Accords would later fill. Yossi Beilin and other Israeli doves repeatedly argued the API should have been engaged as a working draft (Beilin, various). Crucially, the API encoded a sequencing rule — **Palestinian state first, normalization second** — that the Abraham Accords would explicitly invert.

## 3. The Abraham Accords — what was actually signed (2020)

### 3.1 The four bilateral tracks

| Date | Parties | Document | Notable feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| 13 Aug 2020 | Israel–UAE | "Joint Statement" / Treaty of Peace, formalized 15 Sept | First Gulf normalization; Israel froze West Bank annexation in exchange |
| 11 Sept 2020 | Israel–Bahrain | Declaration of Peace, signed 15 Sept on Truman Balcony | Tracked UAE deal closely; widely read as Saudi-greenlighted |
| 23 Oct 2020 | Israel–Sudan | Joint statement (general declaration only) | Sudan signed the Abraham Accords *Declaration* but never the bilateral treaty |
| 10 Dec 2020 | Israel–Morocco | Joint declaration; 2021 follow-on | Restored 2000-suspended ties, plus defense MOU |

The signing ceremony of 15 September 2020 produced three documents: the trilateral **Abraham Accords Declaration** (a short text invoking the "common ancestry" of Abraham and committing parties to peace, tolerance, and regional cooperation), and two bilateral instruments — the **Israel-UAE Treaty of Peace, Diplomatic Relations and Full Normalization** and the **Israel-Bahrain Declaration of Peace** (Trump White House Archives 2020; State Department 2020).

### 3.2 Text vs. side deals — the actual price tags

The published declarations say almost nothing about Palestine beyond a vague reference to a "just, comprehensive and enduring resolution." The real consideration sat in **side payments by Washington**:

- **UAE — F-35 sale.** A roughly $23 billion package including 50 F-35 Lightning II aircraft, MQ-9B drones, and advanced munitions. Israel's "qualitative military edge" objection was managed by parallel U.S. commitments (CRS 2024; MEI 2021). The sale stalled under Biden but was not formally cancelled.
- **Bahrain.** No headline arms deal but a deepening of the existing U.S. Fifth Fleet host arrangement and tacit Saudi cover.
- **Sudan — removal from the State Sponsors of Terrorism list.** Trump rescinded Sudan's SST designation on 14 December 2020, after Khartoum agreed to a $335M settlement for 1998 embassy-bombing victims. Sudan's transitional government framed normalization as a precondition Washington imposed.
- **Morocco — U.S. recognition of Moroccan sovereignty over Western Sahara.** Issued by presidential proclamation on 10 December 2020, reversing decades of U.S. neutrality on the Sahrawi question. The Biden administration kept the recognition but slow-walked operational follow-through (e.g., the planned Dakhla consulate stalled) (MEI 2023).

These side deals are why most analysts — including more sympathetic ones like Hussein Ibish (AGSIW) and Steven Cook (CFR) — treat the Accords as a **transactional bundle, not a peace treaty in the Camp David sense**. There were no Israeli concessions of substance to Arab parties; the consideration was U.S. arms, U.S. recognition, and U.S. de-listings.

## 4. Why the structural shift happened

### 4.1 Iran as common ground

The single most cited driver in the policy literature is **convergent threat perception around Iran**. F. Gregory Gause III argues that classic balance-of-power logic would have predicted a Saudi-Israeli alignment much earlier; what unlocked it was the post-2011 collapse of Arab order plus Iran's expansion through Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, and Yemen, capped by the September 2019 Aramco strikes (Gause, CFR interview; Gause in POMEPS). By 2020, Gulf elites perceived Iran's regional reach — and Washington's seeming withdrawal — as more dangerous than residual Israeli occupation policy. Some analysts called the resulting alignment a "Sunni-Israeli quasi-alliance," though Marc Lynch and others caution that this overstates ideological convergence and understates raw transactional logic (Lynch 2024).

### 4.2 The Trump brokerage

Jared Kushner's "Peace to Prosperity" plan (January 2020) explicitly treated Palestinian statehood as conditional, contingent, and demoted. When Palestinian leaders rejected it, the Trump team pivoted from *resolving* the Israeli-Palestinian conflict to *bypassing* it. The Accords were the operationalization of that pivot. Trump's willingness to spend U.S. policy capital — F-35s, Western Sahara recognition, SST de-listing — is what most observers treat as the proximate cause; without those side deals there is little evidence the UAE or Morocco would have moved in 2020 (Brookings 2021; CFR backgrounder).

### 4.3 Material interests of the Arab regimes

- **UAE**: Tech, AI, water, defense industrial cooperation; positioning Dubai-Tel Aviv as a Mediterranean-Gulf corridor.
- **Bahrain**: Manama's small Sunni-ruled, Shia-majority polity; deeper U.S. and Israeli security guarantees against Iran.
- **Morocco**: Western Sahara legitimation, defense procurement.
- **Sudan**: SST removal, IMF/World Bank re-entry, debt relief.

Hussein Ibish (AGSIW) consistently emphasizes that Gulf elites read the Accords through their **Vision 2030-style economic transformation** logic: diversification away from hydrocarbons requires foreign capital, technology, and regional de-risking, and Israel is a node in that network (Ibish, *The National* and AGSIW commentary).

### 4.4 The regime-public delinking

Marc Lynch's central analytic move (Lynch 2024, "Coming Arab Backlash"; *Foreign Affairs* 2024) is that the Accords were premised on a wager that Arab publics no longer cared enough about Palestine to constrain regimes. He argues Trump and then Biden both bought this premise, which Arab Barometer data show was empirically wrong: in the seven Arab Barometer countries surveyed in 2023-24, support for normalizing with Israel did not exceed 13% anywhere; in Saudi Arabia, opposition rose from 38% (2022) to 68% (Dec 2023–Jan 2024) (Arab Barometer 2024; Robbins in *Foreign Affairs* 2024).

### 4.5 Theorists

- **Marc Lynch** (GW): regime-public gap; warns of backlash.
- **Steven Cook** (CFR): structural drivers, Iran-Sunni alignment, but skeptical the Accords add up to a "regional architecture."
- **Hussein Ibish** (AGSIW): closer to Gulf official framing; emphasizes economic and security logic.
- **F. Gregory Gause III** (Texas A&M): alignment patterns, security dilemma, why the alliance came late.

## 5. What the Accords did to the Palestinian issue

### 5.1 Outside-in vs. inside-out

The Oslo paradigm was **inside-out**: solve the Israeli-Palestinian conflict first, and Arab normalization will follow. The Accords advanced an **outside-in** logic championed by figures like Dennis Ross and Martin Indyk: build a regional Arab-Israeli architecture *first*, and use the resulting leverage to eventually pull Israel toward a Palestinian solution (Ross, various; Indyk, Brookings/CFR commentary). Critics — Khalidi most prominently — argue the outside-in formulation was a fig leaf for *abandonment* rather than sequencing.

### 5.2 Reduced Arab leverage on Israel

By granting normalization without conditioning it on the Palestinian file, the Accords removed the principal Arab card. Israel could deepen settlement, manage Gaza by force, and reject any final-status framework without losing Gulf relationships. Khaled Elgindy (MEI) and Daoud Kuttab have made this point repeatedly: the Accords "rewrote the price" of occupation downward.

### 5.3 PA marginalization

The Palestinian Authority, already hollowed by Abbas's authoritarianism and the rise of Hamas, was structurally sidelined. Khalil Shikaki's PCPSR polls show PA approval collapsing through 2021-2023; an October 2023 PCPSR poll found roughly 80% of West Bank/Gaza Palestinians wanted Abbas to resign (PCPSR 2023; Shikaki, NPR 2024). The Accords did not cause PA decline, but they accelerated its irrelevance.

### 5.4 Hamas's pre-October 7 read

Hamas leadership documents recovered from Gaza (reported by *Wall Street Journal* and Israeli outlets in 2024–25, with caveats about source authentication) suggest Yahya Sinwar in late September/early October 2023 explicitly framed an "extraordinary act" as necessary to derail Saudi-Israel normalization. An August 2022 internal Hamas military-leadership memo reportedly called for "repositioning" against the "broad wave of normalization" (*Times of Israel* 2024; *WSJ* via JNS 2024). These documents are contested — see §7.1 — but if genuine, they place the spoiler logic squarely in Hamas's strategic calculus.

## 6. The Saudi–Israel normalization track, 2021–October 2023

### 6.1 The Biden mega-deal

Biden, despite campaigning on treating Saudi Arabia as a "pariah" after the Khashoggi killing, by mid-2022 had pivoted to pursuing the largest possible Saudi-Israeli deal. By summer 2023, the working framework reported across NYT (Friedman columns), WaPo, and *Wall Street Journal* coverage included:

- A **U.S.–Saudi defense pact**, modeled loosely on the U.S.-Japan or U.S.-Korea treaties, providing security guarantees against Iran.
- A **U.S.-Saudi civilian nuclear cooperation agreement**, including (most controversially) **uranium enrichment on Saudi soil** — a 123 Agreement variant that broke from the UAE "gold standard" (no enrichment) (Brookings 2023; *Axios* 2023).
- Eased U.S. arms export rules.
- A **Palestinian component** — though here sources diverge sharply (see §6.3).

### 6.2 MBS's reported demands

The reported Saudi asks (per NYT, WaPo, FT, ICG's Anna Jacobs) clustered around three pillars:

1. Treaty-grade U.S. security guarantee.
2. Civilian nuclear with domestic enrichment.
3. Some "Palestinian deliverable" — either tangible state-building progress or, more minimally, Israeli commitments to non-annexation.

### 6.3 What MBS actually demanded on Palestine — sources disagree

**Sharp disagreement**:
- *NYT/WaPo* reporting through summer 2023 consistently described the Palestinian component as **modest and negotiable** — language about a "credible pathway" rather than a state.
- Saudi officials, post-October 7, have **publicly hardened** the position: in September 2024 MBS stated on Saudi state TV that the Kingdom would not establish diplomatic relations with Israel without an independent Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as capital (*Times of Israel* 2024; INSS 2026).
- Anna Jacobs (ICG) and others have argued the pre-October 7 Saudi position was already firmer on Palestine than U.S. and Israeli interlocutors publicly conceded.
- Hoover Institution analysis (2025) and some Israeli commentators argue MBS would have settled for cosmetics in September 2023 and only hardened post-Gaza for domestic-pressure reasons.

This disagreement matters: if MBS would have accepted cosmetics, then October 7 changed the *price*; if he never would have, October 7 mainly changed the *timing*.

### 6.4 State of play, October 6, 2023

By the eve of October 7, Friedman and others were openly predicting a deal "by Q1 2024." Negotiating teams were exchanging draft language on the defense pact; the nuclear file was the chief remaining sticking point in Washington. Israel and Saudi Arabia were not yet exchanging direct text but were communicating through U.S. intermediaries. Knesset arithmetic was a constraint — Netanyahu's far-right coalition partners (Smotrich, Ben-Gvir) had publicly opposed any Palestinian statehood language.

## 7. The October 7 rupture

### 7.1 Hamas's calculus and the "spoiler theory"

The proposition that Hamas timed the 7 October attack to derail Saudi-Israel normalization rests on three evidence streams:

1. Captured Hamas internal documents (2022–2023) referencing the threat normalization posed to "the Palestinian cause" (*WSJ*, *Times of Israel*).
2. Sinwar communications cited in Israeli intelligence summaries dated late September / early October 2023.
3. Hamas's own post-attack messaging emphasizing the disruption of "Arab normalization."

Caveats: these documents come almost entirely through Israeli intelligence channels and partisan media; independent forensic verification is limited. ICG, MEI, and CSIS (Jon Alterman, Brian Katulis) have offered more cautious framings — that derailing normalization was likely *one* motivation alongside hostage-taking leverage, prisoner exchange, and disrupting West Bank dynamics, but probably not the singular cause (ICG 2024; MEI post-Oct 7 analyses).

### 7.2 Saudi government and public response

Riyadh's response was carefully calibrated:

- **Suspended** active negotiation tracks with Washington in mid-October 2023 but did not formally cancel.
- **Restored** Saudi-Iranian diplomatic ties (the China-brokered March 2023 deal had already begun a hedge), with Tehran exchanges continuing through the war.
- **Hardened** the public Palestinian-state condition.
- **Joined** the September 2025 Saudi-French "two-state initiative" at UNGA, which produced a wave of European recognitions of Palestine (INSS 2026; *Arab Center DC* 2025).

Saudi public sentiment shifted sharply: opposition to normalization in Arab Barometer rose from 38% in 2022 to 68% by early 2024; a Washington Institute survey in August 2025 found roughly 99% of Saudi respondents viewed normalization negatively (Arab Barometer 2024; INSS 2026).

### 7.3 Where the track stands as of 2026

As of May 2026, normalization is in deep freeze. The second Trump administration (2025–) has openly pursued expansion — including reported overtures to Syria (post-Assad), Lebanon, and Saudi Arabia — and announced Kazakhstan's accession in November 2025 (Heritage 2025; Atlantic Council 2025). Saudi Arabia has publicly **denied** Trump's claim that Riyadh dropped the Palestinian-state precondition (Anadolu Agency 2025). The Saudi position is now: state, then normalization. The API sequencing has, in effect, partially returned — though not the API text.

## 8. Has the Abraham Accords framework survived Gaza?

| Country | Status (2024–2026) |
|---|---|
| **UAE** | Diplomatic ties intact. Public condemnations of Israeli conduct in Gaza, including ICJ-aligned statements. Major bilateral economic projects "paused" or recalibrated; AI/defense cooperation continues quietly. The Emirati framing is "we stay engaged precisely to influence" (Carnegie 2025; Atlantic Council 2025). |
| **Bahrain** | Parliament voted to suspend ties and recall its ambassador in November 2023, but the executive (which controls foreign policy) did not formalize the recall. Defense and intelligence cooperation continues, including reported Israeli drone sales (INSS 2025). |
| **Morocco** | Air links suspended; tourism collapsed. Government held the legal relationship open; the king issued statements distancing Morocco from Israeli Gaza policy. Western Sahara recognition under Trump 2.0 remains intact (Carnegie 2025). |
| **Sudan** | The 2020 framework was always notional. Sudan's 2023 civil war between SAF and RSF froze any operational normalization. |

The Atlantic Council's "Accords at five" assessment (2025) and Carnegie's *Change of Context* paper (2025) converge on a single judgment: the Accords have proved **institutionally durable but functionally diminished**. None of the four states withdrew; all of them downgraded.

## 9. Key debates

### 9.1 Critics — Accords entrench occupation

**Rashid Khalidi** argues that the Accords were part of an effort to "bury the Palestine question" while Israel completes annexationist *de facto* changes on the ground. In his framing (Khalidi 2020, 2023, 2024 *Jacobin*), the Accords are continuous with Oslo: each was sold as a step toward peace but operationally entrenched the occupation. **Ilan Pappé**, working from a settler-colonial framework, treats the Accords as Arab regime collaboration with an ongoing dispossession project. Elias Khoury and Palestinian intellectuals write similarly. Marc Lynch, while not a "rejectionist," reaches a structurally similar conclusion via the regime/public gap.

### 9.2 Defenders — building regional architecture

**Dennis Ross** and **Martin Indyk** defended the Accords as a "regional architecture" play: build economic and security ties first, generate stakes that eventually pull all parties — including a future Palestinian state — into the system. **Tom Friedman** (NYT columns 2020–2023) leaned strongly into the mega-deal narrative, arguing a Saudi-Israel deal could absorb a Palestinian-state component the smaller Accords could not. The Atlantic Council and Washington Institute house views run in this direction.

### 9.3 The "two-track" view

A middle position: regimes have one logic (security, economics, U.S. relationship), publics have another (Palestine, dignity, Arab identity). Arab Barometer data (2024) and the Arab Opinion Index (2025) consistently show populations rejecting normalization at 70–90%+ levels across MENA. The two-track view holds that the Accords are sustainable only as long as authoritarian regimes can absorb the public cost — a wager that becomes more expensive after Gaza (Robbins/Arab Barometer 2024; Arab Center DC 2025).

## 11. Three exam-ready paragraph templates

### Template A — "Continuity or rupture?"

> The Abraham Accords represent both continuity and rupture in the regional system. Continuity, because Gulf-Israeli convergence around the Iranian threat had been quietly building since Iran's post-2003 expansion through Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, and Yemen, culminating in the September 2019 Aramco strikes (Gause). Rupture, because the Accords inverted the sequencing rule that had defined Arab-Israeli diplomacy since the 2002 Arab Peace Initiative: that Arab normalization was the *reward* for Israeli withdrawal and Palestinian statehood. Under the Accords, normalization became the vehicle, and the Palestinian file was demoted to footnote (Lynch 2024). The Trump brokerage made this possible through side payments — the F-35 sale to the UAE, recognition of Moroccan sovereignty over Western Sahara, removal of Sudan from the State Sponsors of Terrorism list — that priced normalization to Arab regimes without requiring Israeli concessions. Whether one judges this a structural shift or a temporary aberration depends on what one thinks October 7 did: my view is that it forced a partial reversion to API-style sequencing, visible in MBS's September 2024 public conditioning of any Saudi deal on a Palestinian state.

### Template B — "What did October 7 do to the Saudi track?"

> By October 6, 2023, the Biden administration was within visible reach of a Saudi-Israeli mega-deal — defense pact, civilian nuclear cooperation, and a still-undefined Palestinian component (Friedman columns; Jacobs/ICG). Hamas internal documents recovered post-attack and Sinwar communications dated late September 2023 indicate that derailing this normalization was, at minimum, one strategic motivation for October 7 — the so-called spoiler theory (*WSJ* 2024; *Times of Israel* 2024; with caveats about source provenance). October 7 produced three immediate effects on the track. First, Riyadh suspended active negotiation while keeping channels open. Second, Saudi public opposition to normalization spiked from 38% (2022) to 68% by early 2024 (Arab Barometer), constraining MBS's room for maneuver. Third, the Palestinian-state condition, previously vague, became explicit: in September 2024 MBS publicly stated no normalization without an independent Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as capital, and Saudi Arabia in 2025 co-led the Saudi-French two-state initiative at UNGA. As of 2026, the track is institutionally alive but substantively frozen, and the API sequencing has partially returned even without the API text.

### Template C — "Did the Accords survive Gaza?"

> Institutionally yes, functionally no. None of the four Abraham Accords signatories — UAE, Bahrain, Morocco, Sudan — has formally withdrawn. Bahrain's parliament voted to suspend ties in November 2023 and recall the ambassador, but the executive declined to formalize the move, and security cooperation including drone transfers continues. The UAE issued sharp public condemnations of Israeli conduct in Gaza, supported ICJ-aligned positions, and paused signature economic initiatives, but kept embassies and intelligence ties open. Morocco saw air links cut and tourism collapse, and the king publicly distanced his government from Israeli policy, but Western Sahara recognition (renewed under Trump 2.0) anchors the relationship. Sudan's notional 2020 framework has been overtaken by the 2023 civil war. The Carnegie and Atlantic Council assessments at the five-year mark (2025) converge on calling the Accords "durable but diminished" — a regional architecture that survived its first major stress test, but only by retreating into a quieter, slower, public-opinion-conscious mode that looks much less like the transformational regional realignment the Trump administration originally claimed.
