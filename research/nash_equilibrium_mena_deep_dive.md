## 1. Executive thesis

A Nash-equilibrium framing illuminates Middle East regional politics by showing why a system everyone calls "broken" is also remarkably *stable*: each major actor — Israel, Iran, the Palestinian factions, Saudi Arabia, the Gulf states, Hezbollah, and increasingly Turkey — is playing a best response to every other actor's best response, so no one can unilaterally change strategy without making themselves worse off, even though the joint outcome is socially destructive (Schelling 1960; Fearon 1995). Hammad's "carousel" metaphor captures the dynamic version: escalation and de-escalation cycle, but the underlying strategy profile does not move. The framing's principal limit is that NE assumes fixed preferences and rational calculation; identity politics, prospect-theoretic loss aversion, domestic-institutional path dependence, and structural-economic forces (oil rents, US security guarantees) all shape the payoff matrix in ways pure game theory underdescribes (Wendt 1999; McDermott 2004; Mitchell 2011). The equilibrium is therefore best read as a *strategic floor*: it tells you why nothing changes on its own, and what kind of shock is required to force change.

## 2. Nash equilibrium 101 — the concept, properly stated

A **Nash equilibrium** is a strategy profile (one strategy per player) such that no player can unilaterally deviate and improve her own payoff, holding every other player's strategy fixed (Osborne and Rubinstein 1994, 14; Tadelis 2013, ch. 5). Three points the exam will reward:

- **Pure vs. mixed strategies.** A pure-strategy NE assigns each player one definite action; a mixed-strategy NE assigns probabilities over actions. Nash's (1950) existence theorem guarantees that every finite game has at least one equilibrium in mixed strategies, but pure-strategy equilibria need not exist (e.g., Matching Pennies has only a mixed NE).
- **NE is not Pareto-optimal.** The classic Prisoner's Dilemma has a unique NE — both defect — that is jointly worse than mutual cooperation. This is the analytic core of Hammad's framing: rational individual choice can produce collectively destructive outcomes (Schelling 1960; Axelrod 1984).
- **Multiple equilibria and coordination problems.** Many games have several NE (e.g., Stag Hunt has two pure equilibria: stag-stag and hare-hare). Which one is selected depends on focal points, history, and beliefs about what others will do (Schelling 1960). In IR, this is why deterrence relationships can flip between a "stable" and "unstable" equilibrium without either side's preferences changing.

Two intuitions to memorize: (i) NE is about *consistency of beliefs and best responses*, not about optimality; (ii) "rational" in NE is thin — it just means each player maximizes given expectations about others.

## 3. Applying NE to international relations — the canonical IR uses

Game theory entered IR through Thomas Schelling's *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960), which reframed deterrence as a coordination problem under threat: nuclear adversaries must converge on tacit rules (focal points, red lines) because explicit bargaining is hard and reneging is cheap. Several downstream concepts the exam may reward:

- **Mutual deterrence as NE.** Two nuclear powers each prefer not to strike given the other's second-strike capability — the classic mutual assured destruction equilibrium. Neither can unilaterally do better by attacking.
- **Stability–instability paradox** (Snyder 1965). Stability at the strategic (nuclear) level produces *instability* at lower rungs: precisely because all-out war is unthinkable, sub-strategic provocations become safer. This is why the Israel-Iran shadow war — drone strikes, cyber, proxy operations — proliferated under nuclear ambiguity.
- **Security dilemma** (Jervis 1978). Defensive measures by State A look offensive to State B, who arms in response, making A genuinely less secure. The offense-defense balance and the ability to *distinguish* offensive from defensive postures determine whether the equilibrium is benign or war-prone.
- **Stag Hunt vs. Prisoner's Dilemma.** Stag Hunt has cooperation as one of its NE — coordination is sufficient to reach it. PD does not — defection is the only NE. Whether the Middle East is "stuck" because actors cannot coordinate (Stag Hunt) or because each is dominated to defect (PD) is itself a contested empirical question (Jervis 1978; Glaser 2010).
- **Relative-gains constraint** (Snidal 1991; Grieco 1988). Realists argue states care not just about absolute payoffs but about how gains are distributed — so even mutually beneficial deals can be blocked if one side gains more.
- **Glaser's rational-choice realism** (2010) formalizes when cooperation is feasible: it depends on the offense-defense balance, signaling capacity, and motivations (greedy vs. security-seeking).

## 4. The MENA application — what makes the region "stuck"?

The Middle East is the textbook case of a many-player asymmetric game with a stable but conflictual NE. Each actor's strategy is a best response given the others'.

- **Israel.** Strategy: security maximalism, nuclear ambiguity (the Begin Doctrine on counter-proliferation, the 1981 Osirak and 2007 al-Kibar strikes), and the "Iron Wall" logic — that only demonstrated unbreakable strength compels Arab acceptance (Jabotinsky 1923; Shlaim 2000). Post-October 7, this hardened: deterrence restoration through overwhelming force in Gaza, the 2024 pager operation against Hezbollah, and direct strikes on Iran. Given every other actor's strategy, unilateral concession is electorally and strategically costly.
- **Palestinian factions.** A *split* equilibrium: Hamas pursues armed resistance partly because it cannot credibly commit to a long peace without losing legitimacy to harder factions; the PA in Ramallah accommodates partly because security cooperation is the price of survival. The Hamas-PA divide is itself an internal NE: each faction's continued existence depends on the other not disappearing (Brown 2003; Pearlman 2011).
- **Iran.** Forward defense via a network of non-state proxies (Hezbollah, Iraqi militias, Houthis, until 2024 Assad), nuclear hedging, and asymmetric deterrence. Given Israeli air superiority and US presence, conventional parity is impossible, so asymmetric and stand-off capabilities are Iran's best response (Ostovar 2016).
- **Saudi Arabia.** Regime survival as the master variable; balance Iran via US security guarantees and selective Israeli cooperation; hedge between Washington and Beijing (the 2023 China-brokered Saudi-Iran rapprochement). Vision 2030 is a payoff-altering project: if economic transformation succeeds, the regime's reliance on regional hegemony decreases.
- **Gulf states (UAE, Qatar, Bahrain).** Economic diversification plus security alignment plus selective normalization (Abraham Accords 2020 for UAE/Bahrain; Qatari mediation as niche strategy).
- **Hezbollah.** Deterrence vis-à-vis Israel via rocket arsenal, plus veto power in Lebanese politics. Severely degraded in 2024; the equilibrium it anchored is now in flux.
- **Turkey.** Increasingly pivotal: backed the HTS-led offensive that toppled Assad in December 2024, expanding influence at Iran's expense.

The point: each strategy is a *best response* to every other strategy. No one can unilaterally moderate without paying a domestic-political and security cost greater than the gain. That is the formal definition of a Nash equilibrium, and it is why the region looks both unbearable and immovable.

## 5. The "carousel" metaphor — Hammad's framing reconstructed

Hammad's "carousel" is a vivid restatement of a *repeated game with stable conflict as its long-run equilibrium*. Episodes of escalation — the 1973 Yom Kippur War, the 1982 Lebanon War, the two intifadas, the 2006 Lebanon war, the Gaza wars of 2008–09, 2012, 2014, 2021, and the October 7 attacks and ensuing Gaza war — give way to de-escalation episodes — Camp David 1978, Madrid 1991, Oslo 1993–95, the 2002 Arab Peace Initiative, ceasefires, the Abraham Accords 2020. Each de-escalation buys time but does not change the strategy profile: settlement expansion continues, refugee status persists, Iran's nuclear program advances, proxies rearm. The carousel rotates; the riders are unchanged.

Game-theoretically, this is the **folk theorem** intuition: in infinitely repeated games, many outcomes — including persistent low-grade conflict punctuated by punishment phases — can be sustained as equilibria if players are patient enough and can credibly threaten retaliation (Axelrod 1984; Fudenberg and Maskin 1986). Each "war round" is a punishment phase that re-establishes the bargaining range; the post-war ceasefire restores the same equilibrium with slightly altered red lines. That is why the underlying structure — disputed sovereignty, asymmetric military balances, externally backed proxies — survives every rotation.

## 6. Why the NE persists — the constraints on deviation

Five mechanisms keep each actor locked into best-response play:

1. **Domestic political costs of unilateral concession.** An Israeli prime minister who cedes settlements without reciprocal security loses the coalition; a Palestinian leader who recognizes Israel without statehood loses legitimacy; an Iranian regime that abandons proxies forfeits its revolutionary identity. Putnam's (1988) two-level games formalize this: leaders must satisfy the domestic "win-set" simultaneously with the international one.
2. **Information asymmetries and commitment problems** (Fearon 1995). States cannot credibly commit to honor a deal once their relative power changes; they have incentives to misrepresent capabilities; some issues (Jerusalem, right of return) appear indivisible. Each blocks the bargaining range that would otherwise dominate war.
3. **Reputation effects** (Mercer 1996). Concession-giving leaders fear adversaries will read it as weakness — though Mercer himself argues this fear is overblown, leaders behave as if reputation transfers across cases.
4. **Audience costs** (Fearon 1994). Public threats create domestic costs for backing down, which paradoxically makes threats more credible — and makes de-escalation harder once leaders have publicly committed.
5. **Lock-in by facts on the ground.** Settlements, refugee populations, Hezbollah's embedded arsenal, Iranian proxy networks, regime-legitimacy narratives — each constitutes a sunk investment that raises the cost of deviation.

## 7. How a NE can shift — the exit conditions (this is Q14)

Hammad's "last slide" almost certainly enumerates the conditions under which the equilibrium ceases to be self-enforcing. Five mechanisms, each with a concrete MENA example:

- **Exogenous shocks that change payoffs.** The 1979 Iranian Revolution overthrew the Shah and converted Iran from an Israeli partner into the region's leading revisionist; the 2011 Arab Spring temporarily destabilized Egypt, Syria, Libya, Yemen; the December 2024 collapse of Assad — enabled by Israel's degradation of Hezbollah and Iran, plus Russian distraction in Ukraine — broke the Iranian "land bridge" to Lebanon and reshaped the Levantine balance.
- **Hegemonic intervention that alters the bargaining range.** Camp David 1978 (Carter brokering Egypt-Israel) removed the largest Arab military from the equilibrium and changed every subsequent payoff. Oslo 1993 created the PA. The Abraham Accords (2020) pulled UAE, Bahrain, Morocco, and Sudan into formal relations with Israel without resolving the Palestinian issue, demonstrating that the equilibrium can partially re-anchor.
- **Domestic upheaval and regime change.** 1979 Iran, 1981 Sadat assassination, 2003 Iraq (externally imposed), 2011 Tunisia/Egypt/Libya, 2024 Syria. Each removed an actor or replaced its preferences and so altered who was playing the game.
- **New information that resolves commitment problems.** Sadat's 1977 Jerusalem visit publicly committed Egypt to recognition in a way no private signal could; the IAEA's Iran inspections regime (when it functions) reduces uncertainty about nuclear intentions. Costly signals collapse private-information explanations for war (Fearon 1995).
- **Coalition shifts and great-power realignment.** The 2023 China-brokered Saudi-Iran resumption of relations; growing US strategic withdrawal from active brokerage; the China-Russia-Iran soft alignment. If the US security guarantee weakens, Gulf states' best response shifts from "align with Washington" toward hedging or accommodation with Tehran.
- **Cost-rebalancing — when status-quo costs exceed cooperation costs.** Walt (1996) argues revolutions and wars reshape threat perceptions and thus alliances. Climate stress, water scarcity, and oil-demand decline could in principle raise the cost of regional militarization above the cost of cooperation, though this is a long-horizon claim.

Each of these is a way of saying: the equilibrium changes when the *payoff matrix* changes, not when actors get tired of conflict.

## 8. Critiques of the NE framing

- **Constructivist objection** (Wendt 1999; Hopf 1998). "Rationality" presupposes given preferences, but preferences are constructed through identity, narrative, and norm. The Israel-Palestine conflict is not just over land and security; it is over competing narratives of indigeneity, historical injustice, and religious meaning. Game theory takes the utility function as exogenous; constructivism endogenizes it.
- **Historical-institutionalist objection** (Pierson 2004). Path dependence — the timing and sequence of British Mandate boundaries, the 1948 refugee creation, the 1967 occupation, the 1979 revolution — locks in institutional arrangements that no current strategic calculation explains. NE describes the present strategy profile but cannot tell you why *this* profile rather than another.
- **Marxist/structural objection** (Mitchell 2011). The Middle East order is shaped less by strategic interaction than by oil rents, the dollar-recycling system, US arms transfers, and the structural role of the region in global energy markets. Reading the equilibrium as a game of states obscures the political economy that constitutes the actors and their resources.
- **Psychological objection** (Kahneman and Tversky 1979; McDermott 2004). Prospect theory shows actors are loss-averse and overweight low-probability extreme outcomes. Leaders defending a "domain of losses" (Israel after October 7; Iran after the 2024 axis collapse) take risks that an expected-utility model rules out. NE assumes risk-neutral or stably risk-averse agents.
- **Empirical-methodological objection.** Many "applications" of NE to the Middle East are post-hoc storytelling — the analyst picks the equilibrium that matches what happened. Without specifying payoffs ex ante, the framework lacks falsifiable content.

These critiques do not destroy the framing — they bound it. NE explains the *structure of strategic incentives*; the critiques explain why those incentives are what they are.

## 9. Possible scenarios going forward (Q15) — using NE language

- **Scenario A: Continuation of the carousel (default).** Each actor continues best-responding; episodic wars and ceasefires; no structural resolution. This is what NE predicts absent shocks.
- **Scenario B: Israeli-Saudi normalization with a Palestinian-state component.** A US-brokered grand bargain that bundles a Saudi-Israel deal with credible Palestinian sovereignty would alter every regional payoff, marginalize Hamas and Iran simultaneously, and constitute a *new* equilibrium. Probability conditional on US engagement and Israeli coalition politics.
- **Scenario C: Iranian regime collapse or major Iran-Israel war.** Removes the principal revisionist anchor and collapses the current equilibrium; the successor equilibrium depends on what replaces the Islamic Republic. The 2024 axis-of-resistance degradation makes this scenario more salient than at any time since 1979.
- **Scenario D: US strategic withdrawal and a multipolar realignment.** China and Russia partially substitute as security underwriters; Gulf states hedge harder; the equilibrium re-anchors around a different external guarantor with different preferences (China prioritizes commerce and stability over democracy promotion).
- **Scenario E: Climate, water, and post-oil shocks.** Long-horizon. Saudi/Gulf transition risk, Iraqi and Levantine water stress, and declining oil demand restructure the payoff matrix from the bottom up; possible failed-state cascades or, alternatively, new cooperation imperatives.

## 11. Two exam-ready paragraph templates

### Q10 template — introducing Nash equilibrium (≈250 words)

A Nash equilibrium is a configuration in which each player's strategy is a best response to every other player's strategy, so no actor can unilaterally change course and improve her payoff (Osborne and Rubinstein 1994). Crucially, a Nash equilibrium need not be Pareto-optimal — the Prisoner's Dilemma's unique equilibrium is mutual defection, jointly worse than mutual cooperation. This is the core of why the framework illuminates Middle East regional politics. Israel, Iran, the Palestinian factions, Saudi Arabia, the Gulf states, and Hezbollah are each pursuing what they perceive as rational strategies given everyone else's: Israel maintains security maximalism and nuclear ambiguity rooted in the "Iron Wall" logic (Shlaim 2000); Iran conducts asymmetric deterrence through proxies because conventional parity is impossible; Hamas pursues armed resistance partly because it cannot credibly commit to long peace without losing legitimacy; Saudi Arabia hedges between Washington and Beijing while balancing Iran. None of these actors can unilaterally moderate without absorbing domestic-political and security costs greater than the gain — Fearon's (1995) trio of commitment problems, private information, and issue indivisibility blocks the bargaining range that would otherwise dominate war. The result is what Professor Hammad calls the carousel: episodes of escalation (the October 7 attack and the Gaza war) and de-escalation (Abraham Accords, ceasefires) without changing the underlying strategy profile, because each actor's best response remains constant. The equilibrium is socially destructive but individually rational — which is exactly why Schelling (1960) treated such configurations as the central problem of strategic studies.

### Q14 template — how the equilibrium can end (≈250 words)

A Nash equilibrium ceases to hold when the payoff matrix itself changes, not when actors merely tire of conflict. Five mechanisms force such change. First, exogenous shocks: the 1979 Iranian Revolution converted Iran from Israeli partner to leading revisionist; the December 2024 collapse of Assad — enabled by Israel's degradation of Hezbollah and Russian distraction in Ukraine — broke Iran's land bridge to Lebanon and reshaped the Levantine balance. Second, hegemonic intervention: Camp David 1978 removed Egypt, the largest Arab military, from the equilibrium and altered every subsequent payoff; Oslo 1993 created the Palestinian Authority; the Abraham Accords 2020 pulled four states into recognition without resolving the Palestinian core. Third, domestic upheaval and regime change reshape preferences and even who is playing the game. Fourth, new information resolves commitment problems — Sadat's 1977 Jerusalem visit was the canonical costly signal that private signaling could not have produced (Fearon 1995). Fifth, coalition shifts and great-power realignment alter the external constraint: the 2023 China-brokered Saudi-Iran restoration and ongoing US strategic recalibration are pushing Gulf states from clean alignment toward hedging. Walt (1996) generalizes: when status-quo costs exceed cooperation costs, the equilibrium becomes vulnerable. The carousel rotates within the current equilibrium; an exit requires a shock to the matrix. The current moment — post-October 7, post-Assad, mid-realignment — is unusually shock-rich, which is why an old equilibrium may be ending even though the next one is not yet visible.
